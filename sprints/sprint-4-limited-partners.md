## 4.1 — The Importance of Limited Partners
Source: https://govclab.com/2023/08/16/the-importance-of-limited-partners/
(`vcl.to/VI4-1` is a dead shortlink; the article is live)

**A limited partner is a passive investor in a fund.** They put in the money and take no part in
running it. The framing that actually changed how I read this: LPs are the fund's **customers**.
A GP is not primarily an investor of their own money — they are a steward of someone else's, paid
a fee to do it, and legally bound by fiduciary duty to act in that person's interest.

### How the money got here
- **1822** — New York passes the first US limited partnership statute, borrowed from the French
  *commandite* system, where a silent partner could back a venture with liability capped at what
  they put in.
- **1979** — the Department of Labor swaps the "prudent man rule" for the "prudent investor rule,"
  and pension funds can finally buy risk. This is the single regulatory change that funded
  Silicon Valley.
- **1990 → 2000** — US venture investment goes from roughly $3B to $105B (NVCA).
- **Post-2008** — LPs start demanding real data and clearer reporting, and move toward
  co-investing to cut fees and get more control.

### What LPs actually do, beyond the money
Set return expectations · nudge strategy if the stake is big enough · act as a feedback channel ·
hold the authority to force a fund into limited operations if duties are breached · sit on the
**LPAC** · and turn up to events, which makes them a community rather than a cap table.

### Good LP / bad LP
Good: trustworthy, transparent, long-term, gives actionable rather than just critical feedback,
flexible. Bad: impatient for returns, opaque or shifting terms, goals misaligned with the fund,
burdensome admin demands, overzealous interference. The article's warning is blunt — the wrong
LPs can sink a fund, so this is a screen that runs both ways.

### What LPs expect
Strong returns · a **clear strategy** they can repeat back · transparent communication including
the bad quarters · alignment, meaning the GP has their own money in · and real governance.

### Note to self
I write personal checks, so right now **I am the LP.** That makes the good/bad traits list
uncomfortably useful as a list about myself. Impatience, opacity about my own reasoning, goals
that drift from the stated thesis — those are my failure modes before they are ever someone
else's, and nobody is going to put me into limited operations.

The other thing to take now rather than later: of the five LP expectations, exactly one can be
built before there is a fund, and it is **clear strategy**. Returns need checks, alignment needs a
GP commit, governance needs an entity. A thesis sharp enough that an LP could repeat it back needs
none of that — it needs the anti-thesis work, which is the thing already half-done in
`thesis.md`. That is the LP-readiness work available to me today.

## 4.2 — Limited Partner Archetypes
Source: https://govclab.com/2023/08/16/lp-archetypes/
(`vcl.to/VI4-2` is a dead shortlink; the article is live, titled "Targeting the Right LPs")

A five-step motion: identify the archetypes that fit · build a persona for each · build a target
list of real names · test receptiveness on a small sample · refine, and drop what is not working.

**Plausible for a Fund I:** personal friends · angel investors · **alumni and affinity networks**
· tech executives · small business owners · exited founders · high-net-worth individuals · family
offices · bankers · corporations and corporate VC · other VC firms · next-generation wealth ·
funds of funds.

**Unlikely for a Fund I:** sovereign wealth funds · endowments · insurance companies · pension
funds. Useful to have that said out loud — it is a list of doors not to spend a year knocking on.

The step that matters most is the first, and the article gives a hard test for it: **target
archetypes where you already have dozens of first- and second-degree connections.** Everything
downstream is wasted if that is not true.

### Note to self
The archetype on that list that is mine is **alumni and affinity networks** — Plug and Play,
Grid110, Material Change. And it is the same asset `thesis.md` already names as my dealflow edge,
which means the map I owe myself does double duty: the pressure-test item "map which alumni
relationships are live vs. dormant" is the dealflow map *and* the first LP target list. One
evening's work, two jobs.

Worth being honest about the order, though. Those relationships route me deals today and might
route me LPs in three years. If I spend them asking for money before I have a track record, I
spend the dealflow edge to buy an LP conversation I am not ready to have. Dealflow first.

The last step is the one I would otherwise skip: **know when to stop pursuing an archetype.** Some
produce only small checks, some lose interest. Stopping is a decision, not a failure — the same
thing the dealflow sessions said about a pass.

## 4.3 — Sourcing and Closing LPs
Source: https://govclab.com/2023/08/16/sourcing-and-closing-limited-partners/
(`vcl.to/VI4-3` is a dead shortlink; the article is live)

### The LP construction model (example: $10M fund)

| Check size | Commitments needed | Typical archetype |
|---|---|---|
| $1M | 2 | Family offices, funds of funds, corporate VC |
| $500K | 6 | Exited founders, tech executives |
| $250K | 12 | HNWIs, bankers, larger VC firms |
| $100K | 20 | Friends, alumni networks, angels |

**Initial close** typically captures 10–25% of target fund size.

### The funnel
Source → **Connector** intro → initial meeting → pitch → materials → **PACT signed** →
diligence → legal review → agreement signed → capital call. Eleven steps total.

The **PACT** is the checkpoint that matters: a non-binding commitment letter that hard-circles
an LP as serious. Ask for it the moment genuine interest appears.

### Note to self
"Identifying real LPs requires searching with a combination of less obvious keywords" — the
obvious searches surface the same over-solicited names everyone else found.

## 4.4 — Maintaining Relationships with LPs
Source: https://govclab.com/2023/08/16/maintaining-lp-relationships/
(`vcl.to/VI4-4` is a dead shortlink; the article is live)

The goal is stated as one sentence: a relationship durable enough that they invest in the **next**
fund too. So this session is really about retention, and it has actual instruments.

### Signals, both directions
**Working:** they reply to updates · they fund capital calls quickly · they show up to things ·
they say they are in for the next one.
**Breaking:** replies slow down · capital calls drag · questions turn into scrutiny · and the one
I would not have thought of — **back-channeling**, going around official updates for information,
which reads as a trust problem rather than a curiosity problem.

### Four rules for how to write to them
The article gives each one a good and a bad version, and the bad versions are the ones worth
copying down.

- **Transparent** — "one of our key investments underperformed this quarter due to market
  volatility," not "there are some ups and downs in our portfolio."
- **Matter of fact** — "XYZ Corp added 5,000 users this month," not "the new portfolio company is
  skyrocketing."
- **Data rich** — "the portfolio was marked up 1.7x last quarter, driven by XYZ Corp," not "we've
  had substantial growth."
- **Clear** — "we invested in a promising biotech startup," not "we backed a leader in the
  neurospatial hardware vertical, a B2B2C play."

Cadence runs from brief email updates and scheduled calls through monthly newsletters and LP
dinners up to quarterly reports and an annual gathering. The tips worth keeping: be consistent,
set boundaries on what LPs decide, and **avoid surprises** — nobody should learn about an exit
after the fact.

### Note to self
There is no fund, so there are no LPs, and almost all of this still applies to me now. The four
communication rules are a writing standard, and I can point them at the three places I already
write: founder updates, this notebook, and anything I eventually send an LP. The fourth bad
example is the exact kind of sentence my own working agreement bans, which is a useful
coincidence — the rule I set for this repo and the rule VC Lab teaches for LP reporting are the
same rule.

**Avoid surprises** is the one that maps straight onto a brand value I already wrote down.
Transparency, in `background.md`, is "saying the real thing, including when it's inconvenient."
This session is the operational version of that: the inconvenient thing has to arrive early, from
me, in plain numbers — and the failure signal is somebody going around me to find out.
