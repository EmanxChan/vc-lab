## The Power Law

Venture returns aren't spread evenly across a portfolio — one investment typically returns more
than every other investment combined. That single fact drives everything about how a fund is
built: you construct a portfolio expecting most companies to fail or go sideways, so the job
isn't picking safe winners, it's making sure you're in the one company capable of returning the
whole fund. It also means fund size and check size have to be set so that a single outlier can
actually move the fund's numbers.

## MOIC, TVPI, and IRR

- **MOIC** (Multiple on Invested Capital) — the raw multiple on what was put in. Simple, but
  ignores time.
- **TVPI** (Total Value to Paid-In) — distributions *plus* remaining portfolio value, divided by
  capital paid in. The headline "how is the fund doing" number, blending realized and unrealized.
  Splits into **DPI** (cash actually returned) and **RVPI** (paper value still in the ground).
- **IRR** — the annualized, time-weighted rate of return. It's the one that lets LPs compare a VC
  fund against other asset classes, but it flatters early markups and can be gamed by timing.

Together: TVPI tells you the size of the outcome, DPI tells you how much is real, IRR tells you
how fast you got there.

## "2 and 20" — alignment and tension

The GP charges roughly 2% of committed capital annually to run the firm — salaries, diligence,
travel — and takes 20% of profits as carried interest, usually only after LPs get their capital
back plus a preferred return (~8%). The alignment is real: carry means the GP only gets
meaningfully rich if the LPs do. The tension is that management fees are paid regardless of
performance, so a large fund can pay its GPs well even if it never returns capital — which is
why LPs scrutinize fund size, and why carry (not fees) is what a serious emerging manager
points to.

## My market and stage: do they produce outliers?

My focus is AI and humanity at pre-seed to seed in the United States, and I think the honest
answer is yes on stage and "yes, with a caveat" on market. Pre-seed and seed is precisely where
power-law economics work in a fund's favor: entry ownership is cheap, so a single company
reaching a large outcome can return the entire fund, which is much harder to achieve when you're
paying Series A prices for de-risked companies. AI is also the clearest platform shift of this
decade, and the US is where the largest outcomes in that shift are being priced. The caveat is
my "humanity" filter. Companies applying AI to health, education, labor, and civic life often
face regulated buyers, longer sales cycles, and acquirer sets that top out in the hundreds of
millions rather than the tens of billions — so the distribution of outcomes may be wider and
shallower than in pure infrastructure or developer tooling. What that means for my approach is
that fund size is the variable I have to get right, not thesis conviction: I should size the
fund small enough that a $300–500M exit still returns it, target meaningful ownership at entry
rather than spraying small checks, and hold real reserves so I can keep buying into the one
company that does break out. I'd rather run a fund where several good outcomes produce a strong
return than one that quietly requires a decacorn to work at all.
