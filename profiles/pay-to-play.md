## Why it matters

Pay-to-play forces investors to support the company in a hard round or lose their protections.
Investors who don't participate pro rata have their preferred converted to common, stripping the
preference and the anti-dilution.

It exists because down rounds reveal who is actually committed. An investor who wants downside
protection but won't put in more money is taking a position the other investors have to fund.

## What it looks like in practice

Provisions vary in severity: full conversion to common is the harshest, while some convert only
the non-participating portion or move investors to a shadow series with reduced rights.

It appears most often in down rounds and recapitalisations, and it's frequently negotiated at
that moment rather than being present from the start. That timing matters — it's a term added
under pressure.

## Where it goes wrong

- Assuming an existing investor will participate. Funds run out of reserves, or out of
  conviction.
- Not understanding what the conversion strips — often anti-dilution as well as the preference.
- Applying it without a realistic view of which investors can actually follow on.
- Reading a pay-to-play round as routine. It's a signal about the company's position.
