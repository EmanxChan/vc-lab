## Why it matters

Some technical debt is correct. A pre-seed company that builds for a scale it may never reach
has optimised for the wrong risk — most startups die from not finding customers, not from bad
architecture.

The failure is not taking on debt, it's taking it on unconsciously and never paying any of it
down. Debt taken deliberately with a repayment plan is leverage. Debt accumulated by accident is
how a team's velocity halves without anyone deciding it should.

## What it looks like in practice

The signal that it's gone too far is velocity: features that used to take days taking weeks,
bugs reappearing, engineers routing around parts of the system. That's the point where paying
down is cheaper than continuing.

In diligence it shows up as a question about what would break at 10x. A CTO who can name the
three things they'd have to rebuild has a real picture. One who says the architecture scales
fine usually hasn't looked.

## Where it goes wrong

- Treating all technical debt as bad, and over-engineering a product that hasn't found fit.
- Taking on debt without recording it, so nobody knows where the landmines are.
- Rewriting from scratch instead of paying down incrementally.
- Letting debt accumulate until velocity collapses, then trying to explain the slowdown to a
  board.

## Where the term comes from

Ward Cunningham coined the metaphor in 1992, and later stressed the part people drop: the debt
is worth taking as long as you pay it back. The borrowing was never the problem.
