## Why it matters

ROFR gives the company and its investors the ability to control who ends up on the cap table. If
a shareholder wants to sell, the company and then existing investors get the chance to buy first
on the same terms.

The purpose is to keep shares out of the hands of competitors, unknown parties, or anyone whose
presence would complicate future rounds. The cost is that it makes any private sale slower and
more visible.

## What it looks like in practice

The standard NVCA structure runs in sequence: the company has first refusal, then investors, and
whatever remains is subject to co-sale rights. Each step has a defined notice period.

In practice ROFR often prevents a sale simply by making it slow. A buyer who has to wait through
two refusal windows to find out whether the deal survives frequently doesn't wait.

## Where it goes wrong

- Not budgeting time for the refusal windows when planning a secondary sale.
- Assuming founder shares are exempt. Often they aren't.
- Letting the company hold ROFR without the cash to exercise it, which just delays everything.
- Confusing ROFR with a right of first offer, which works differently.
