## Why it matters

Early adopters are the only customers available to a company that hasn't built the thing yet, so
who they are shapes everything: what gets built, what the pricing anchors to, and what the first
case studies say.

They are also the biggest source of false signal. Early adopters tolerate things the mainstream
will not, and they buy for reasons the mainstream doesn't share. A product tuned entirely to
them can stall the moment it tries to cross over.

## What it looks like in practice

You find them by looking for people already trying to solve the problem badly — the ones with a
homemade spreadsheet, a contractor on retainer, a Zapier chain held together with hope. That
effort is a budget and an urgency that already exists.

The distinction that matters later is whether the early adopters bought *because* the problem is
acute, or because they enjoy trying new tools. The first group predicts a market. The second
group predicts nothing.

## Where it goes wrong

- Mistaking enthusiasm for novelty with genuine need. Some people just like new software.
- Building every feature the early adopters ask for and ending up with a product only they can
  use.
- Assuming the mainstream buyer will accept the same rough edges. They won't.
- Never asking why they were willing to go first. The answer is the sharpest version of your
  pitch.
