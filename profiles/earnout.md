## Why it matters

Earnouts exist to bridge a disagreement about value, and they resolve it by transferring risk to
the seller. The seller keeps the upside case they were arguing for, and takes the chance that it
doesn't happen under someone else's management.

The structural problem is that the person responsible for hitting the targets no longer controls
the resources. Earnouts are among the most common sources of post-acquisition litigation for
exactly this reason.

## What it looks like in practice

The terms that make an earnout achievable are about control and measurement: what resources are
guaranteed, how the acquired business is accounted for separately, what happens if it's
reorganised or merged into another unit, and precisely how the metric is calculated.

Revenue-based earnouts are more achievable than profit-based ones, because profit can be reduced
by allocated overhead the seller doesn't control. Simple, countable metrics beat sophisticated
ones.

## Where it goes wrong

- Accepting an earnout on profit, which the acquirer can affect through cost allocation.
- Not securing the resources needed to hit the targets in writing.
- Failing to address what happens if the business is reorganised or the product is discontinued.
- Valuing the company at the full earnout, when most of it goes unpaid.
