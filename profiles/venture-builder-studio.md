## Why it matters

A venture studio creates companies rather than investing in them — generating the idea,
assembling the team, and holding a much larger stake than an investor would. Typically 30–50%
rather than 10–20%.

The model trades portfolio breadth for ownership depth. Studios produce fewer companies and own
much more of each, which changes the arithmetic of the power law considerably.

## What it looks like in practice

The operational reality is that studios are expensive to run — they carry the cost of building
companies internally, not just evaluating them. That cost has to be funded from somewhere,
usually a combination of fund economics and services revenue.

The recurring difficulty is founder recruitment. A talented founder can start their own company
and keep most of the equity, so studios have to offer something that justifies the difference.

## Where it goes wrong

- Applying investor portfolio math to a studio, where the ownership and cost structure differ.
- Underestimating how hard it is to recruit strong founders into a large-dilution structure.
- Building companies the studio can staff rather than ones the market wants.
- Treating studio equity as equivalent to founder equity when incentives differ.
