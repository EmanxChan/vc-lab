## Why it matters

Vesting is what stops a co-founder who leaves after five months from owning a third of the
company forever — which is the single most destructive thing that can happen to a cap table,
because it makes every future round harder to raise.

Investors require founder vesting for exactly this reason, and often reset it at the first
priced round even for founders who have been there for years. That reset feels punitive and
isn't; it's the price of everyone else's confidence that the team stays.

## What it looks like in practice

Four years with a one-year cliff and monthly vesting thereafter is the near-universal default
for both founders and employees. Deviations exist — longer schedules at later-stage companies,
shorter for advisors — but the default is what gets assumed unless stated.

For founder stock the mechanism is usually a repurchase right: the founder owns all the shares
immediately, and the company can buy back the unvested portion if they leave. That structure is
what makes the 83(b) election both possible and important.

## Where it goes wrong

- Founders skipping vesting on themselves because they trust each other. The trust isn't the
  point.
- Setting no cliff, so someone who leaves in month two keeps something.
- Forgetting that a vesting reset at the first priced round is normal, and treating it as an
  insult.
- Not documenting the schedule, so the leaver and the company remember it differently.
