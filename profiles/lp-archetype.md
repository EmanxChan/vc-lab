## Why it matters

An LP list sorted by name is a list of strangers. An LP list sorted by archetype is a list of
arguments, because everyone inside one archetype wants roughly the same thing and is reachable
roughly the same way. An exited founder is buying proximity to the next wave. A family office is
buying diversification with a long horizon. A corporate is buying early sight of technology it
might acquire. The same fund, pitched three ways.

The practical consequence is that archetype selection happens before outreach, not during it,
and it is decided by your network rather than by your preferences.

## What it looks like in practice

VC Lab's five-step motion: identify the archetypes that fit the thesis, write a persona for each,
build a target list of real names, test receptiveness on a small sample, then refine and drop what
is not working.

The qualifying test is connection density — target archetypes where you already have dozens of
first- and second-degree relationships. Sovereign wealth funds, endowments, insurance companies
and pension funds are explicitly flagged as unlikely for a Fund I, which is useful mostly as a
list of doors not to spend a year knocking on.

The last step is the one people skip. Some archetypes reliably produce small checks, some lose
interest halfway through. Stopping is a decision to make deliberately, the same way a pass is.

## Where it goes wrong

- Picking archetypes by prestige rather than by how many of them you can actually reach.
- Writing one pitch and sending it to every archetype on the list.
- Spending a warm network on a fundraise before there is a track record to show it.
- Treating "unlikely for Fund I" as a challenge rather than as information.
- Never deciding to stop, so a dead archetype quietly absorbs a year of outreach.
