## Why it matters

Market size is the term investors most expect to be inflated, so an inflated number costs
credibility rather than buying it. A $90B TAM assembled from an industry report signals that the
founder hasn't done the work.

What the number is actually for is checking whether the outcome can be large enough to matter to
a fund. Under the power law, a company that can only ever be worth $60M is not a fit for a fund
needing one investment to return everything — regardless of quality.

## What it looks like in practice

The credible version is built bottom-up: number of potential customers times realistic annual
contract value. That produces a smaller number and a much better conversation, because every
input can be examined.

SOM is where the argument actually lives. It forces a statement about the go-to-market — how
many customers can be reached, at what rate, through what channel — which is the part that
reveals whether the founder has a plan.

## Where it goes wrong

- Quoting an analyst report's industry figure as TAM.
- The 1% fallacy — "if we capture 1% of a $50B market" — which describes no plan at all.
- Defining TAM so broadly it includes people who will never buy this.
- Presenting only TAM and skipping SAM and SOM, where the real constraint is.
