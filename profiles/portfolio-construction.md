## Why it matters

Portfolio construction is decided before the deals arrive and determines what the deals can
return. Number of positions, diligence depth per position, and reserve ratio are three dials set
in advance, and together they set the shape of every outcome the fund can have.

The three named shapes are a useful shorthand. Focused buys conviction and pays for it in
diversification. Dispersed buys the sector and accepts it cannot help any single company much.
Hybrid splits the difference and pays for it by requiring expertise in more places.

## What it looks like in practice

Focused: a small number of thesis-driven checks, deep diligence, a board seat, and traditionally
around 50% of the fund held in reserve for follow-ons. Dispersed: many companies inside one
sector, light diligence, support delivered through standard resources, 10% or less reserved.
Hybrid: a medium number, moderate diligence, advisory-board involvement.

Two caveats matter more than the taxonomy. First, new managers with small funds frequently cannot
reserve meaningfully at all, so the 50% figure describes large funds rather than first ones.
Second, the choice is partly a time budget: the real constraint on a concentrated portfolio is how
many founders one person can be useful to.

A narrow thesis can also decide this for you. If a filter rejects 28 of 29 companies, there is not
enough passing the screen to build a dispersed portfolio from, so the concentration arrives as a
consequence rather than a strategy — along with its risk.

## Where it goes wrong

- Choosing a shape by preference, then discovering the thesis only supports a different one.
- Quoting the 50% reserve figure on a fund too small to honour it.
- Dispersing widely while still promising hands-on support.
- Concentrating without naming the diversification risk that comes with it.
- Setting the reserve ratio without modelling the dilution path to exit.
