## Why it matters

A secondary sale is an existing shareholder selling to a new buyer, rather than the company
issuing new shares. It provides liquidity without a company exit, which matters when companies
stay private for a decade.

For a fund approaching the end of its life it's often the only way to realise a position in a
company that isn't selling and isn't going public.

## What it looks like in practice

Transfer restrictions govern what's possible: rights of first refusal, co-sale rights, and board
consent all apply, and each adds delay. A sale that looks straightforward can take months.

Secondaries usually price at a discount to the last round, partly because the buyer gets common
or a less favourable class, and partly because the seller needs the liquidity more than the
buyer needs the position.

## Where it goes wrong

- Assuming shares can be sold freely. Almost always they can't.
- Not budgeting for the ROFR and co-sale windows.
- Reading a secondary price as the company's valuation. The terms and class differ.
- Ignoring the effect on the 409A when a large secondary establishes a common price.
