## Why it matters

Companies stay private far longer than they used to, which means employees can hold illiquid
paper for a decade. Tender offers exist to release some of that pressure without an exit, and
they've become a standard retention tool at later-stage companies.

They're structured rather than ad hoc for good reasons: uncontrolled secondary sales create a
messy cap table, unknown shareholders, and price signals the company doesn't control.

## What it looks like in practice

The company sets the terms — who is eligible, what proportion can be sold, and at what price —
and the buyer is usually an incoming or existing investor. Because the transaction establishes a
price for common stock, it can affect the 409A valuation and therefore future option strike
prices.

Eligibility rules typically require a minimum tenure and cap the percentage sellable, so the
process provides relief without removing the incentive it was designed to preserve.

## Where it goes wrong

- Running one before the company can support the resulting 409A increase.
- Allowing unrestricted selling, which removes the retention value entirely.
- Ignoring the tax treatment for employees, which differs from an exit and can surprise people.
- Letting uncoordinated secondary sales happen instead, and losing control of the cap table.
