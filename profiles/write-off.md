## Why it matters

Write-offs are the expected case, not the exception. Under the power law most positions return
nothing, and a portfolio without write-offs usually means the manager wasn't taking enough risk
to catch an outlier.

The discipline is timing. Carrying a dead company at cost for years overstates the portfolio and
delays the honest conversation with LPs.

## What it looks like in practice

A position is written off when there's no realistic prospect of recovery — the company has
ceased operations, or the preference stack makes the position worthless regardless of outcome.

The second case is the one that gets missed. A company still operating can be worth nothing to
an early investor if later rounds stacked preferences above them.

## Where it goes wrong

- Carrying dead positions at cost to protect the reported TVPI.
- Writing off only on formal shutdown, ignoring positions where the stack makes recovery
  impossible.
- Reading write-offs as failures of judgment rather than as the expected distribution of
  outcomes.
- Not learning anything from them, which is the only thing they're actually good for.
