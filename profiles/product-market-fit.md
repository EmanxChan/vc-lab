## Why it matters

Product-market fit is the dividing line that determines what a company should be doing. Before
it, the job is learning — talking to users, changing the product, sometimes changing who it's
for. After it, the job is scaling what already works.

Scaling before fit is the classic way to die with money in the bank: spending to acquire users
who don't stay, at a rate that only accelerates the loss.

## What it looks like in practice

The signals are behavioural rather than declarative. Users return without prompting, get upset
when it breaks, tell colleagues unasked, and usage grows when nobody is pushing it. Compare that
with a product where every user traces back to founder outreach and retention decays quietly
after week one.

Fit is also segment-specific. Strong fit in one narrow segment and none in another is the normal
shape, and the mistake is averaging across them.

## Where it goes wrong

- Treating it as binary rather than as a gradient that varies by segment.
- Reading enthusiasm as fit. People are generous, especially to founders they like.
- Reading signups as fit. Retention is the signal; acquisition isn't.
- Assuming it's permanent. Fit is relative to a market, and markets move.

## Where the term comes from

Marc Andreessen's 2007 post "The Only Thing That Matters" defined the term in one line — being
in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market — while explicitly attributing the
underlying rule to Andy Rachleff, then of Benchmark, as "Rachleff's Corollary of Startup
Success."
