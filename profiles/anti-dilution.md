## Why it matters

Anti-dilution protects an investor's price, not their percentage — a distinction that matters.
If the company later sells stock more cheaply, the earlier investor's conversion price adjusts
downward, so they get more shares for the same money.

The cost falls on common shareholders. The adjustment doesn't create value; it moves ownership
from founders and employees to the protected investor at exactly the moment everyone is already
demoralised.

## What it looks like in practice

Full ratchet reprices the earlier investment entirely to the new, lower price — punitive, and
rare outside distressed situations. Broad-based weighted average is the market standard,
adjusting partially based on how much new stock is issued and at what price.

The difference between broad-based and narrow-based is which shares count in the denominator.
Broad-based includes options and convertibles, produces a smaller adjustment, and is more
founder-friendly.

## Where it goes wrong

- Accepting full ratchet, which can transfer enormous ownership in a modest down round.
- Not knowing whether the weighted average is broad- or narrow-based.
- Assuming it protects percentage. It protects price.
- Forgetting that pay-to-play provisions can switch it off for investors who don't participate.
