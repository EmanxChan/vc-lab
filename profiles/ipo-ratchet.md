## Why it matters

An IPO ratchet guarantees a late-stage investor a minimum return at IPO by adjusting their
conversion ratio if the offering prices below a threshold. If the IPO disappoints, they get more
shares to compensate.

It's a late-stage term that appears when a company raises at a valuation the public market may
not support, and it transfers that risk to everyone else on the cap table.

## What it looks like in practice

The mechanism is the same as anti-dilution but triggered by the IPO price rather than a private
round. Below the threshold, additional shares are issued to the protected investor, diluting
common and earlier preferred.

Its presence is informative in itself. A ratchet suggests the last round priced above what the
investor was confident the market would bear.

## Where it goes wrong

- Accepting a ratchet to preserve a headline valuation in a late round.
- Not modelling what the ratchet costs common at a range of IPO prices.
- Reading a high late-stage valuation without checking what structure supports it.
- Assuming ratchets only exist in down rounds. They're a forward-looking protection.
