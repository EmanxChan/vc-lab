## Why it matters

Observer seats let a company give investors visibility without expanding the board, which
matters because board size is one of the few things that gets worse monotonically. Every
additional voting member makes decisions slower.

They're not free, though. Observers still consume preparation time, still ask questions, and
still change what gets said in the room — and unlike directors they owe the company no fiduciary
duty.

## What it looks like in practice

Rights are usually limited to attending and receiving materials, with the company able to
exclude the observer from sessions covering conflicts, litigation, or privileged legal advice.
That carve-out is worth having explicitly.

The number accumulates quietly. Granting one per round produces a Series B board meeting with
four directors and five observers, which changes the meeting from a working session into a
performance.

## Where it goes wrong

- Granting observer rights freely, then finding the room is too crowded to have a real
  conversation.
- Omitting confidentiality obligations for observers.
- Not reserving the right to exclude them from privileged or conflicted sessions.
- Giving an observer seat to a fund that competes with a portfolio company.
