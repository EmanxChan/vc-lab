## Why it matters

The investment period is when new investments can be made — typically the first three to five
years. The fund's total life is usually ten years plus extensions, and everything after the
investment period is management and harvest.

These dates drive a lot of behaviour that looks strange from the outside: why a GP is suddenly
eager to deploy, why pressure for exits appears around year eight, and why a great company can
be sold earlier than it should be.

## What it looks like in practice

After the investment period, management fees typically step down and only follow-on investments
in existing companies are permitted. Extensions past year ten usually require LPAC or LP
consent, often granted in one-year increments.

The mismatch between a ten-year fund and a company that takes twelve years to exit is
structural, and it's why secondary sales and continuation vehicles exist.

## Where it goes wrong

- Deploying quickly at the end of the investment period to avoid leaving capital uninvested.
- Modelling a fund's return without accounting for the pressure to exit within its life.
- Assuming extensions are automatic.
- Forgetting that fee step-downs change the management company's budget significantly.
