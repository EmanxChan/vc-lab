## Why it matters

The deck is the artifact that circulates without you, so it has to survive being read by someone
who wasn't in the room and has ninety seconds. That's a different design problem from
presenting.

It's also a thinking test more than a design one. Vague decks usually indicate vague thinking,
and investors read them that way — which is why a beautifully designed deck with no specific
claim reads worse than an ugly one with sharp numbers.

## What it looks like in practice

The conventional sequence works because it maps to how the decision gets made: problem, why now,
solution, market, traction, business model, competition, team, and the ask. Deviating is fine;
skipping the questions isn't.

The traction slide does most of the work at pre-seed, and when there's little to show, the team
and the why-now carry it. Specific beats impressive: three real customers named beats a large
chart of projections.

## Where it goes wrong

- Leading with the solution before establishing that the problem is expensive.
- Top-down market sizing that produces a number nobody believes.
- Hiding weak traction behind percentages with no absolute numbers.
- Making it a reading document, so there's nothing left to talk about.
