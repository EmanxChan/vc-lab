## Why it matters

Blended retention mixes customers who joined at different times under different products, which
makes it nearly useless in a company that's changing. A growing company's blended number is
dominated by recent joiners and looks better than the business is.

Cohorts are also the only honest basis for LTV. A retention curve that flattens — where some
share of each cohort stays indefinitely — is a fundamentally different business from one that
decays to zero, and you cannot tell which you have from an average.

## What it looks like in practice

Plot each cohort's survival against age rather than calendar date, so cohorts are comparable.
What you're looking for is whether later cohorts sit above earlier ones at the same age, and
whether the curve flattens.

The flattening point is the most important feature. A curve that stabilises at 40% means a
durable base; one that keeps declining means the business has to keep replacing everyone,
forever.

## Where it goes wrong

- Reporting blended retention in a company that's growing quickly.
- Comparing cohorts by calendar date instead of by age.
- Judging a cohort before it's old enough to show the shape of the curve.
- Measuring logo retention only, when revenue retention tells a different story.
