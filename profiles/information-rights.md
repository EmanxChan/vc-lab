## Why it matters

Information rights are the mechanism by which investors without a board seat stay informed.
They're also an obligation on the company that recurs forever, which is why they're usually
limited to investors above a threshold.

The cost is real but small when handled properly. Companies that run a disciplined monthly
update satisfy their information rights as a by-product; companies that don't end up answering
the same questions individually.

## What it looks like in practice

Standard rights cover annual audited or reviewed financials, quarterly unaudited financials, an
annual budget, and reasonable inspection rights. Major-investor thresholds keep the obligation
manageable.

Inspection rights are the ones worth reading. Broad access to books and records can be
burdensome and, with a strategic investor, occasionally sensitive.

## Where it goes wrong

- Granting full rights to every small holder, creating an unmanageable reporting burden.
- Ignoring them until an investor formally requests financials you don't produce.
- Giving broad inspection rights to an investor with a competing portfolio company.
- Treating them as a substitute for actually communicating with investors.
