## Why it matters

Conversion is what makes preferred stock work in both directions: downside protection through
the preference, full upside through conversion. Investors take whichever is worth more, which is
why the preference only binds in modest outcomes.

Understanding the conversion point — the exit value where converting beats taking the preference
— tells you which regime you're in, and that changes who is aligned with whom in an acquisition
negotiation.

## What it looks like in practice

Automatic conversion is triggered by a qualified IPO, defined by a minimum offering size and
sometimes a minimum price, or by a vote of a specified majority of preferred. That vote
threshold is the term worth reading closely.

It matters because a small group of holders can otherwise block a conversion the rest of the
company needs, which is the mechanism behind most hold-up situations at IPO or acquisition.

## Where it goes wrong

- Ignoring the automatic conversion thresholds until an IPO is actually on the table.
- Setting the qualified IPO bar so high that no realistic offering clears it.
- Assuming all preferred converts together when each series may vote separately.
- Forgetting that anti-dilution adjusts the conversion ratio, not just the price.
