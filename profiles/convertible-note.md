## Why it matters

The convertible note predates the SAFE and does the same job with more machinery: it's debt, so
it carries interest and a maturity date. Those two features are the whole difference, and they
matter when things go badly.

A maturity date is a real obligation. If the company hasn't raised a priced round by then, the
note is technically due — which in practice means a renegotiation, an extension, or a conversion
on whatever terms can be agreed under pressure.

## What it looks like in practice

Notes remain common in bridges and in markets where the SAFE never fully took over. The economic
terms mirror SAFEs — cap, discount — with interest accruing and converting alongside principal.

The maturity date is the term to watch in diligence. A company with notes maturing inside the
next year has a deadline that will shape its fundraising whether or not the founder frames it
that way.

## Where it goes wrong

- Ignoring maturity dates until they arrive, when the leverage sits entirely with the holder.
- Forgetting accrued interest converts too, which increases dilution beyond the principal.
- Using a note where a SAFE would do, and paying for the extra complexity.
- Assuming holders will always extend. Usually they do, and the terms change.
