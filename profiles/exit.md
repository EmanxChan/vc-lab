## Why it matters

The exit is the only event that converts a position into money. Everything before it — markups,
valuations, TVPI — is an estimate of what an exit might produce.

It's also the constraint the fund structure imposes on the company. A ten-year fund needs exits
within its life, which is why GPs push for liquidity on timelines that sometimes have nothing to
do with what's best for the business.

## What it looks like in practice

The routes are acquisition, IPO, secondary sale, or acqui-hire, and the distribution is heavily
skewed toward acquisition. IPOs are rare and getting rarer as companies stay private longer.

What each holder receives depends on the waterfall, not the headline price. A well-publicised
exit can pay common shareholders nothing if the preference stack exceeds the proceeds.

## Where it goes wrong

- Modelling exit value without running it through the preference stack.
- Assuming an IPO is a realistic base case. For almost all companies it isn't.
- Reading an announced acquisition price as what shareholders received.
- Ignoring that fund life creates pressure to exit that the company doesn't share.
