## Why it matters

The memo forces the argument to be written down, which is where vague enthusiasm either becomes
a thesis or falls apart. Writing is the diligence — the gaps become visible when you try to make
the case in prose.

It's also the artifact that makes learning possible. A memo written at the time, including the
case against and what would falsify the thesis, is what lets you evaluate your own judgment
years later.

## What it looks like in practice

The section that matters most is the case against. A memo that only argues for the investment
isn't analysis, it's advocacy — and the risks named honestly at the time are what make a later
post-mortem useful.

Length is not the point. A tight two pages with a clear thesis, the evidence, the risks, and the
falsifiers beats fifteen pages of description.

## Where it goes wrong

- Writing the memo to justify a decision already made.
- Omitting or softening the case against.
- Not stating what would falsify the thesis, which makes it unfalsifiable.
- Never revisiting old memos, which is where the entire learning value sits.
