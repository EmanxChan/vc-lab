## Why it matters

Switching costs cut both ways, and founders usually only think about one. High switching costs
protect you once you're in — that's most of what a moat actually is in software. They also stand
between you and every customer you don't have yet.

For an early company the incumbent's switching costs are the real competitor. Being 30% better
rarely clears them. Being 10x better on one specific thing sometimes does, which is why wedges
exist.

## What it looks like in practice

Add up what actually has to happen: exporting data that may not export cleanly, rebuilding
integrations, retraining staff, running both systems in parallel, and one person staking their
reputation on it. That last one is usually the binding constraint.

The strategies that work are the ones that lower the cost rather than argue with it — doing the
migration yourself, running alongside the incumbent instead of replacing it, or entering through
a workflow nobody has automated yet.

## Where it goes wrong

- Assuming a better product is enough to overcome them. It usually isn't.
- Ignoring the political cost — someone has to own the decision and wear the failure.
- Building switching costs so high they become the reason customers resent you.
- Forgetting that your own customers face the same arithmetic when they consider leaving.
