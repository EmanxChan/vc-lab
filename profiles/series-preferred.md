## Why it matters

Each financing round issues a new series — Series A Preferred, Series B Preferred — each with
its own price, preference, and rights. They're not interchangeable, and the differences between
them decide who gets paid first.

The complexity compounds. By Series C there may be four series with different preferences,
different anti-dilution, and different voting requirements, and any of them may be able to block
a transaction.

## What it looks like in practice

The two structural questions are seniority — whether later series get paid before earlier ones —
and whether series vote together as one class or separately.

Voting separately gives each round a veto, which becomes progressively harder to manage. Voting
as a single class is simpler and more common in well-structured cap tables.

## Where it goes wrong

- Assuming all preferred is equivalent when modelling a waterfall.
- Not knowing whether series vote together or separately.
- Letting each round negotiate its own bespoke terms, so the stack becomes unmanageable.
- Modelling only the most recent series and ignoring the ones underneath it.
