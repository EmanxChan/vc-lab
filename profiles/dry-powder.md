## Why it matters

Dry powder is capital committed but not yet deployed — the fund's remaining capacity. It
determines what a fund can still do, both in new investments and in defending existing
positions.

It matters most in a downturn. A fund with dry powder can support its portfolio through a hard
period and buy at lower prices; a fully deployed fund can only watch.

## What it looks like in practice

The number to track is uncalled commitments minus expected future fees, split between what's
earmarked for new investments and what's reserved for follow-ons.

Industry-wide dry powder is also a market signal. Large accumulated dry powder tends to support
valuations because the capital has to be deployed within the investment period, regardless of
whether prices are attractive.

## Where it goes wrong

- Counting all uncalled capital as available, ignoring future management fees.
- Deploying dry powder late in the investment period just because the clock is running.
- Not distinguishing between dry powder for new deals and reserves for follow-ons.
- Assuming a portfolio company's other investors have reserves. Often they don't.
