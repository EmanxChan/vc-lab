## Why it matters

The SAFE exists because pricing a company with no revenue is guesswork, and paying lawyers to do
it properly costs more than the round justifies. It defers the valuation to the first priced
round and lets the money arrive in days rather than months.

The trade is that dilution becomes invisible until it lands. A founder who has sold five SAFEs
at different caps often does not know what they own, and the reckoning arrives all at once at
conversion.

## What it looks like in practice

Y Combinator introduced the SAFE in 2013 and moved to the post-money version in 2018. The change
matters: post-money SAFEs fix the investor's percentage, so all the dilution from subsequent
SAFEs falls on the founder rather than being shared.

The right discipline is to model conversion continuously — every SAFE sold, at every cap,
against a realistic next-round price. That is a spreadsheet exercise that prevents a very common
surprise.

## Where it goes wrong

- Stacking SAFEs at different caps without modelling the combined conversion.
- Not knowing whether the SAFE is pre- or post-money, which changes who bears the dilution.
- Treating the cap as a valuation. It's a ceiling on conversion price, not a price.
- Raising so much on SAFEs that the priced round starts with most of the company already spoken
  for.
