## Why it matters

Automatic conversion forces all preferred into common on a qualifying event, usually an IPO
above a defined size. Without it, a company couldn't go public with a preference stack intact,
because public markets won't price around one.

The threshold and the voting requirement are what matter. Set them wrong and a minority of
holders can block the conversion the whole company needs.

## What it looks like in practice

The trigger is typically a qualified IPO — a minimum offering size and sometimes a minimum price
— or a vote of a specified majority of preferred, often voting as a single class.

Where series vote separately, each one has a veto, which is precisely the structure that
produces hold-up problems at the moment of exit.

## Where it goes wrong

- Setting a qualified IPO threshold so high no realistic offering clears it.
- Allowing series-by-series votes on conversion, creating multiple vetoes.
- Not checking the threshold until an IPO is being planned.
- Assuming automatic conversion covers acquisitions. Usually it covers IPOs and votes.
