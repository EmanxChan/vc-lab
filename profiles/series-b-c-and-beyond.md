## Why it matters

Later rounds are where the preference stack gets heavy and where early investors find out
whether their ownership survived. Each round adds dilution and adds its own preference ahead of
common, which is why the payout math at exit can look nothing like the cap table.

For a pre-seed investor these rounds mostly matter as markups and as dilution. Without pro rata
rights and reserves, a position that started at 8% can be well under 3% by the time anything is
realised.

## What it looks like in practice

The character of the rounds changes as they go: growth funds care about efficiency metrics
rather than potential, and the diligence shifts from judgment to arithmetic. By Series C the
company is being valued on numbers a spreadsheet can check.

The average US unicorn raises more than six rounds before it gets there, which is the practical
argument for reserves — the companies that work will ask for money many more times.

## Where it goes wrong

- Assuming a markup is a return. It's an unrealised number until there's a distribution.
- Not modelling the preference stack that has accumulated by the later rounds.
- Failing to reserve for follow-ons, and being diluted out of the one that works.
- Reading a large round as validation without looking at the terms attached to it.
