## Why it matters

Winning a category you defined is the strongest position in business — you set the evaluation
criteria and every competitor is measured against your shape. It's also expensive, slow, and
mostly attempted by companies that should have just competed.

The hidden cost is education. Existing categories come with existing budget, existing search
behaviour, and buyers who already know why they need one. A new category has none of that, and
creating it is a marketing spend most pre-seed companies can't fund.

## What it looks like in practice

It works when the existing category genuinely can't describe what you do, and when the buyer's
problem is real but unnamed. It fails when it's a repositioning exercise on a product that is,
in fact, a slightly better version of an existing thing.

A pragmatic middle path most good companies take: sell into an existing category so the budget
exists, and describe the new category as the reason you're better at it. Anchor to what they
know, differentiate on what they don't.

## Where it goes wrong

- Creating a category to avoid an unflattering comparison rather than because the comparison is
  wrong.
- Underestimating the education cost, which is usually years and a marketing budget.
- Naming a category nobody searches for, so inbound never arrives.
- Refusing to be compared to anything, which leaves the buyer with no way to evaluate you.
