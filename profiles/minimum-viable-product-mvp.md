## Why it matters

The point is learning speed, not thrift. An MVP that takes nine months isn't minimum, and an MVP
that can't produce a clear yes or no isn't viable. Both failures are common, and the second is
more expensive because it feels like progress.

Framing it as "smallest testable version of the riskiest assumption" fixes most of the
confusion. Different companies have different riskiest assumptions, so their MVPs look nothing
alike.

## What it looks like in practice

Start by naming the assumption that would kill the company if wrong, then build only what tests
it. If the risk is whether people want it, a landing page and a waitlist might do. If the risk
is whether it's technically possible, you have to build the hard part first.

Concierge and Wizard-of-Oz versions — humans doing manually what software will eventually do —
are usually the fastest route to a real answer, and they produce better information than a
prototype because someone is actually being served.

## Where it goes wrong

- Building a small version of the full product instead of a test of the main risk.
- Making it so minimal that a bad result tells you nothing about the real idea.
- Shipping an MVP with no defined question, so the result gets interpreted to taste.
- Treating the MVP as v1 of the codebase rather than something you expect to throw away.

## Where the term comes from

The term predates Lean Startup — Frank Robinson coined it around 2001 — but Eric Ries
popularised it in *The Lean Startup* (2011) as the version of a product that enables a full turn
of the build-measure-learn loop with the least effort.
