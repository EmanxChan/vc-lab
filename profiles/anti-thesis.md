## Why it matters

A thesis that only says yes cannot be tested. Every investor can name what they like; far fewer
can name the company that clears every stated criterion and still gets a no. The anti-thesis is
that second list, written down before the deal is in front of you, when saying no is still cheap.

It also does the practical work a thesis is supposed to do. A four-word thesis turns the people
around you into deal scouts — but only if it excludes something. If everything AI-adjacent
qualifies, the network routes you noise and you spend the relationship capital sorting it.

## What it looks like in practice

The useful version is derived rather than invented. Screen a real batch, sort the passes into
groups, and name the reason each group failed — the anti-thesis is the list of those reasons,
and each one carries the company that taught it.

The hardest entries are the near misses: right sector, right stage, right geography, and still
wrong. Those are the ones that prove the filter exists, because rejecting an obvious mismatch
proves nothing.

Each exclusion should come with a boundary line you can state in one sentence, and the company
on either side of it. "Does this change how two people regard each other, or only how one party
regards a machine?" is a line you can apply in a first meeting; "is this really trust?" is not.

## Where it goes wrong

- Written at a desk instead of derived from screens, so it excludes only strawmen.
- No named companies, which means the boundary has never been tested against a real one.
- So tight it excludes the outlier — the counterweight to an anti-thesis is an anti-portfolio.
- Never revisited after the first ten passes, when the actual pattern in your nos becomes visible.
- Used to justify a decision after the fact rather than to make it beforehand.
