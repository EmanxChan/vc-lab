## Why it matters

It reframes the competitive set, which is the part founders get wrong most often. If you only
look at companies that build what you build, you will miss that most of your losses are to a
spreadsheet, an intern, or doing nothing at all.

It also changes what you build. A feature request is a proposed solution; the job is the reason
behind it. Founders who chase requests build a cluttered product. Founders who chase the job
build a coherent one.

## What it looks like in practice

The useful question isn't "what do you want?" It's "walk me through the last time you dealt with
this." What triggered it, what they tried, what they gave up on, what made the current
workaround tolerable enough to keep using.

In practice you're listening for the moment of struggle and what they hired to solve it. When
someone says they use a Google Sheet and three Slack reminders, that stack is the incumbent —
and it's free, familiar, and already integrated into how they work.

## Where it goes wrong

- Turning it into a template exercise instead of listening to how people actually describe
  struggle.
- Writing the job at the level of the feature ("send invoices faster") rather than the progress
  ("get paid without chasing people").
- Ignoring the emotional and social parts of the job. Nobody wants to look disorganised in front
  of their boss.
- Using it to justify a product you already built, rather than to find out what to build.

## Where the term comes from

The framing is Clayton Christensen's, developed at Harvard Business School and set out most
fully in *Competing Against Luck* (2016), though he had been teaching it for years before — the
milkshake study dates to work with Bob Moesta in the mid-2000s. Christensen's line was that
customers don't buy products, they hire them to do a job.
