## Why it matters

This is the term that decides who actually gets paid, and it routinely overrides
what the cap table appears to promise. Ownership percentage tells you how the
proceeds split *after* the preference stack is satisfied — not before. In a big
exit that distinction evaporates, because every investor converts to common and
takes their percentage. In a modest exit it is the whole story.

Founders negotiate hard on valuation and treat the preference as boilerplate.
That is backwards. A higher valuation with a 2x participating preference is
usually worse for the founder than a lower valuation with 1x non-participating,
and the gap widens the worse the outcome gets.

## What it looks like in practice

A company raises $20M across its life and sells for $30M. With a 1x
non-participating preference, investors take their $20M back, and $10M is left
for founders and employees to split. Investors could instead convert to common
and take their percentage of $30M — they take whichever is larger, so at this
price they take the preference.

Make it participating and the arithmetic changes: investors take the $20M *and*
their percentage of the remaining $10M. Roughly $2M more leaves the common pool.
Stack a 2x preference on the last round and the $30M sale can clear the
preference stack entirely, and common gets nothing at all — on a company that
just sold for $30M.

## Where it goes wrong

- Reading the cap table as a payout schedule. It is an ownership record. The
  waterfall is what pays.
- Winning on valuation and losing on structure. Participation and multiples are
  where the real money moves.
- Only modeling the good exit. Run the $30M case, not just the $300M case — the
  preference terms are irrelevant in one and decisive in the other.
- Forgetting the stack compounds. Each round adds its own preference ahead of
  common, and they do not disappear.

## Where the term comes from

Brad Feld and Jason Mendelson walked through venture term sheets clause by
clause on Feld Thoughts across 2005 — price, liquidation preference, board
composition, protective provisions, anti-dilution — at a point when almost
nobody was explaining these terms to founders in public. The liquidation
preference post remains the plainest account of how the economics actually pay
out, and the series became the book *Venture Deals*.
