## Why it matters

It's the fastest predictor of sales cycle length, churn, and how the company does in a downturn.
Painkillers get bought with existing budget and urgency attached. Vitamins need a budget created
for them, which means a longer cycle, more stakeholders, and first place on the cut list when
spending tightens.

Vitamins can build enormous companies — but usually on consumer scale and habit, not on
enterprise budget. The mistake is selling a vitamin with a painkiller's go-to-market.

## What it looks like in practice

The tell is what the customer was doing before. If they had built a fragile internal workaround,
hired someone to do it manually, or were paying for something they complained about, the pain is
real and pre-budgeted. If the honest answer is "nothing, we just lived with it," you are a
vitamin.

Another tell is who initiates. Inbound requests with a deadline attached signal pain.
Enthusiastic meetings that never reach procurement signal a vitamin.

## Where it goes wrong

- Believing enthusiasm proves pain. People are generous with praise and stingy with budget.
- Selling a vitamin as if it were a painkiller and then blaming the sales team for slow cycles.
- Missing that the same product is a painkiller for one segment and a vitamin for another — that
  difference is often the whole ICP.
- Assuming pain is permanent. Regulation changes, and so does what hurts.
