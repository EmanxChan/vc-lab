## Why it matters

The board is where the decisions that a shareholder vote can't reach get made — hiring and
firing the CEO, approving budgets, authorising a sale. A board seat is a fundamentally different
thing from an ownership percentage.

Directors also owe fiduciary duties to the company and all shareholders, not to the fund that
appointed them. That's a real constraint and it's the reason investor directors sometimes vote
against their own fund's short-term interest.

## What it looks like in practice

Early boards are usually three to five people: one or two founders, the lead investor, and an
independent. The independent seat matters more than founders expect, because it breaks deadlocks
and is often the deciding vote in a crisis.

Board size grows with rounds and gets worse as it grows. A seven-person board turns working
sessions into presentations, which is why tying seats to ownership thresholds rather than
granting them permanently is worth negotiating.

## Where it goes wrong

- Granting a permanent seat rather than one tied to continued ownership.
- Expanding the board with every round until it can't have a real conversation.
- Leaving the independent seat empty, so there's no tiebreaker.
- Confusing a board seat with control. Protective provisions can matter more.
