## Why it matters

It is the cheapest way to be wrong before it costs anything. A month of interviews can kill an
idea that would have taken a year and a seed round to disprove.

For an investor, how a founder runs these is a direct read on how they'll handle every other
kind of evidence. Founders who interview to confirm what they already believe will read their
metrics the same way.

## What it looks like in practice

The good version is almost entirely about the past. What happened, when, who was involved, what
it cost, what they tried first, why they stopped. Past behaviour is data. Future intention is
politeness.

You are listening for specifics that a person could not invent on the spot — names of tools,
dollar amounts, the week it went wrong. Vague agreement is the sound of someone being nice to
you.

## Where it goes wrong

- Pitching. The moment you describe your solution, the interview is over and a sales call has
  begun.
- Asking about hypothetical futures instead of actual pasts.
- Only talking to people who like you. Friendly interviews are the least informative kind.
- Stopping after five conversations because the first five agreed.

## Where the term comes from

The discipline comes out of Steve Blank's customer development work at Stanford and Berkeley in
the 2000s — the argument that startups fail from lack of customers, not lack of product, and
that founders should "get out of the building." Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* (2013) is the
practical companion on how to ask without fishing.
