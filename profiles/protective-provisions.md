## Why it matters

This is where control actually lives, and founders consistently underweight it because it isn't
expressed as a percentage. You can own 70% of your company and still be unable to sell it.

The provisions are individually reasonable — no investor wants a company sold out from under
them — and collectively they're the difference between a founder who can act and one who has to
negotiate every significant decision.

## What it looks like in practice

The standard NVCA list covers liquidation or sale, changes to the charter or bylaws, creating
senior securities, increasing the option pool, changing board size, and taking on significant
debt. That set is broadly accepted as market.

The negotiation is about what gets added beyond it, and about who votes. A single class vote
across all preferred is very different from each series voting separately — the latter gives
every round its own veto, which compounds painfully by Series C.

## Where it goes wrong

- Reading only the board and ownership terms and skipping this section.
- Agreeing to series-by-series votes, so each round adds another party who can block.
- Letting the list expand into ordinary operating decisions like hiring or budget.
- Assuming a friendly investor won't use them. The document outlives the relationship.
