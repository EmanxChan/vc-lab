## Why it matters

Timing separates the companies that work from the identical companies that failed years earlier.
Most good ideas have been tried; what changed is usually a cost curve, a regulation, a
behaviour, or a platform.

For a pre-seed investor it's close to the whole thesis. You're not underwriting current traction
because there isn't any — you're underwriting a bet that a specific window just opened and this
team is positioned in it.

## What it looks like in practice

A real why-now names a specific change with a date: inference cost fell by an order of
magnitude, a regulation took effect, a platform opened an API, a behaviour became normal. "AI is
changing everything" is not a why-now; it's a press release.

The strongest version also explains why the incumbent can't simply respond — usually because the
change breaks an assumption their product or business model is built on.

## Where it goes wrong

- Citing a general trend rather than a specific, dated change.
- Having no answer for why this failed before, when it usually did.
- Confusing a technology becoming possible with customers becoming ready.
- Assuming that if the timing is right, the window stays open. It's usually short.
