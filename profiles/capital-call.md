## Why it matters

LPs commit capital but don't hand it over up front. It's drawn down as needed, which is what
makes IRR the metric it is — money that sits with the LP isn't costing them return.

The mechanism also creates a real risk: an LP who can't fund a call is in default, and default
provisions are severe. In a liquidity crisis, defaults cluster exactly when the fund most needs
the money.

## What it looks like in practice

Calls typically give ten business days' notice and are made pro rata across LPs. The LPA
specifies what happens on default — usually forfeiture of a large share of the defaulting LP's
interest.

Some funds use subscription credit lines to bridge calls, which smooths cash flow for LPs and
flatters IRR by delaying the start of the clock. ILPA has pushed for disclosure of returns both
with and without the line for this reason.

## Where it goes wrong

- Calling capital far ahead of deployment, which drags on IRR.
- Not understanding the default provisions until an LP misses a call.
- Using a credit line to flatter IRR without disclosing its effect.
- Assuming committed capital is available on demand. Notice periods are real.
