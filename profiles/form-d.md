## Why it matters

Almost everything in sourcing depends on somebody choosing to tell you. Form D does not. A company
raising on a private exemption has to notify the SEC, and that notice is public the day it posts,
which makes it the one channel that does not route through a warm introduction.

For an angel without a fund's brand, that asymmetry is the point. The filing will not tell you
whether a company is good. It will tell you that a company exists, in your geography, that just
closed money — weeks before a press release and sometimes months before anyone writes it up.

## What it looks like in practice

The filing is short. It names the issuer and its address, the executive officers and directors,
the exemption claimed (almost always Rule 506(b) or 506(c)), the total offering amount, the amount
sold to date, and the date of first sale. Filed on EDGAR, normally within 15 days of that first
sale.

Used as a sourcing tool, it works as a geography-plus-date filter: pull filings with an in-state
address from the last 60 to 90 days, drop the funds and the real-estate vehicles, and what remains
is a list of local companies that just took money. 506(b) versus 506(c) is worth noting — 506(c)
permits general solicitation, so those issuers are allowed to talk publicly about raising, which
usually means they will answer a cold note.

## Where it goes wrong

- Treating the amount sold as the round size. It is the amount sold **as of the filing date**, so a
  rolling SAFE can look like a $250K raise when the target is $2M.
- Assuming a filing means the round is open. Plenty of Form Ds post after a round is full.
- Missing the companies that never file, or file late. Enforcement is thin and small raises slip
  through, so absence of a filing proves nothing.
- Reading the officer list as the founding team. It is who signed, not who matters.
- Mistaking the signal for a thesis fit. A filing is a first-level filter input — geography and
  recency — and nothing more. The company still has to clear every other screen.
