## Why it matters

A liquidity event is any moment shareholders can convert holdings into cash. It's broader than
an exit — secondary sales and tender offers create liquidity for some holders without ending the
company's independence.

The distinction matters because companies stay private far longer than they used to. Partial
liquidity has become a normal part of a long private life rather than something that only
happens at the end.

## What it looks like in practice

The charter's definition of a deemed liquidation event is what determines whether preferences
trigger. Not every liquidity event is one — a secondary sale between shareholders usually isn't.

For employees the practical forms are tender offers and secondary sales, both controlled by the
company and both subject to the transfer restrictions in the shareholder agreements.

## Where it goes wrong

- Assuming every liquidity event triggers liquidation preferences.
- Confusing partial liquidity with an exit for fund accounting purposes.
- Ignoring transfer restrictions that make a private sale impossible without consent.
- Treating a tender offer as equivalent to an exit for the employees who participate.
