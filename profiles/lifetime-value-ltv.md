## Why it matters

LTV is the most confidently quoted and least reliable number in early-stage decks, because it's
a projection built on a retention curve that hasn't happened yet. Small changes in assumed churn
produce enormous changes in LTV.

The 3:1 LTV/CAC benchmark gets repeated as though it were a law. It's a rule of thumb from
mature SaaS, and applying it to a company with eleven months of history is arithmetic dressed as
evidence.

## What it looks like in practice

Use gross profit rather than revenue, and be explicit about the churn assumption — ideally from
measured cohorts rather than an average. Where retention hasn't been observed long enough, the
honest move is to report the payback period instead and say so.

Segmented LTV is more useful than blended, because retention usually varies far more between
segments than within them, and the segment with strong retention is the one worth acquiring more
of.

## Where it goes wrong

- Computing LTV on revenue rather than gross profit.
- Assuming a retention curve far longer than anything observed.
- Quoting an LTV/CAC ratio without stating the churn assumption underneath it.
- Treating a projection as a measurement in a board deck.
