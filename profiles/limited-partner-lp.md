## Why it matters

LPs supply essentially all the capital and take essentially none of the decisions. That
asymmetry is the whole structure: they are buying a manager's judgment for a decade, with almost
no ability to change course once committed.

Understanding who the LPs are explains most GP behaviour. A fund backed by endowments behaves
differently from one backed by family offices, because the reporting expectations, the tolerance
for illiquidity, and the pressure for distributions are all different.

## What it looks like in practice

LPs are typically pensions, endowments, foundations, funds of funds, family offices, and high-
net-worth individuals. Their liability is limited to what they commit, and that limitation
depends on them staying out of management — which is why the LPAC is advisory rather than
decision-making.

For an emerging manager the practical reality is that the first fund is usually raised from
people who know you personally. Institutional LPs generally want to see a prior fund before
committing to one.

## Where it goes wrong

- Treating LPs as a single constituency. Their time horizons and liquidity needs differ
  enormously.
- Promising liquidity a ten-year fund structure cannot deliver.
- Under-communicating between capital calls, so the only contact is a request for money.
- Assuming an LP who committed to Fund I will commit to Fund II. Re-up rates are earned.
