## Why it matters

Syndicates let a small cheque behave like a large one. The organiser gets to write a meaningful
amount into a deal they sourced, the company gets one line on the cap table instead of a dozen,
and the participants get access they couldn't reach individually.

The economics matter: the organiser usually takes carry on the syndicate, which makes this a way
to build a track record and an investor base before raising an actual fund.

## What it looks like in practice

Mechanically this is usually an SPV — a single-purpose vehicle that holds the investment, with
the participants as members. That structure is what keeps the company's cap table clean and
gives the organiser a defined role.

The reputational dynamic is the thing to understand. A syndicate lead is putting their judgment
in front of their network repeatedly, which is a much faster feedback loop than a fund's ten-
year cycle.

## Where it goes wrong

- Syndicating a deal you haven't diligenced, on the strength of who else is in it.
- Ignoring the SPV's fees and carry when calculating the return to participants.
- Building a syndicate of people who never actually fund their commitments.
- Assuming syndicate access substitutes for the relationship a lead has with the company.
