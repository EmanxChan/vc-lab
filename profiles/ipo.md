## Why it matters

An IPO converts private shares into publicly traded ones. For a fund it's rarely an immediate
liquidity event, because lock-ups prevent selling for months after listing.

It's also the trigger for automatic conversion of preferred into common, which is why the
qualified IPO thresholds in the charter matter — set wrong, they can block the offering the
company needs.

## What it looks like in practice

Companies stay private much longer than they did, which has pushed IPOs later and made them
rarer as a share of exits. That change is what created the market for secondary sales and tender
offers.

After the lock-up expires the fund either sells into the market or distributes shares in kind to
LPs. Both have market-impact and timing consequences.

## Where it goes wrong

- Treating an IPO as an immediate liquidity event. The lock-up usually runs 90–180 days.
- Marking a position at the offer price and treating it as realised.
- Not checking whether the qualified IPO threshold in the charter is realistically achievable.
- Distributing in kind to LPs who cannot hold public securities.
