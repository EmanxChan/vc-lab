## Why it matters

NPS is widely used because it's cheap and comparable, and widely over-trusted for the same
reasons. It measures stated sentiment, not behaviour, and stated sentiment and renewal behaviour
diverge more than people expect.

For an early company it's usually the wrong thing to optimise. Retention and usage tell you what
NPS is trying to approximate, more accurately and without asking anyone.

## What it looks like in practice

The value is almost entirely in the free-text follow-up, not the number. "Why did you pick that
score?" produces the actionable material; the score itself just sorts whose answer to read
first.

Sample bias is the standard trap: the people who respond are the ones with strong feelings, and
a 6% response rate makes the resulting number close to meaningless across periods.

## Where it goes wrong

- Treating NPS as a growth metric. It's a sentiment survey.
- Comparing your score to a cross-industry benchmark. The distributions aren't comparable.
- Surveying at moments of peak satisfaction and reporting the result as typical.
- Reading the number and skipping the comments, which is the only part that tells you what to
  do.
