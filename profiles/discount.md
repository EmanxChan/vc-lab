## Why it matters

A discount is the simplest version of the same idea as a cap: money that arrived earlier
converts cheaper. Where a cap protects against a very high round price, a discount just gives a
fixed percentage advantage regardless of price.

On its own a discount is weak protection. If the company does extremely well and prices the next
round very high, a 20% discount on a huge number is still a huge number — which is exactly the
scenario a cap exists to handle.

## What it looks like in practice

Typical discounts run 10–25%, with 20% the common default. In a bridge, discounts are often the
main compensation for taking the risk of going in before the round is settled.

Where both a cap and a discount are present, the investor takes whichever gives more shares.
Rarely both, and the drafting should say so explicitly.

## Where it goes wrong

- Relying on a discount alone in a company that might price very high.
- Assuming discount and cap stack unless the document says they do.
- Setting different discounts across a stack of notes, which complicates conversion for no
  benefit.
- Treating the discount as the whole negotiation when the cap matters more.
