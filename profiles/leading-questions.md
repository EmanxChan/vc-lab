## Why it matters

This is the specific mechanism by which founders fool themselves. It doesn't feel like bad
research while it's happening — it feels like enthusiastic validation, because that's exactly
what it produces.

It matters to an investor because a deck full of "92% of respondents said they would use this"
usually means the survey was written to produce that number. The right follow-up is always: what
was the question, and what did they do afterwards?

## What it looks like in practice

The fix is to ask about behaviour that already happened, and to stay quiet. "What do you use
today?" beats "would you switch?" "How long did that take?" beats "would saving time be
valuable?"

Silence does a lot of the work. Founders fill pauses by suggesting the answer; the discipline is
to let the other person fill it instead.

## Where it goes wrong

- Describing your solution and then asking whether it's a good idea.
- Asking about willingness to pay in the abstract — the answer is always a polite yes.
- Counting hypothetical yeses as demand, then putting the number in a deck.
- Interviewing to find agreement rather than to find the flaw.
