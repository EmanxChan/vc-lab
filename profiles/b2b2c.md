## Why it matters

It solves distribution and creates concentration in the same move. Instead of acquiring
consumers one at a time, you acquire them in blocks — which is enormously efficient and means a
handful of contracts control most of your revenue.

The structural problem is that the payer and the user are different people with different
interests. The employer wants cost control and reporting. The employee wants the thing to be
good. Build only for the buyer and engagement dies; build only for the user and you don't renew.

## What it looks like in practice

The businesses that work here serve both masters explicitly — usage dashboards and ROI reporting
for the buyer, a product the end user would choose voluntarily. Engagement rate is the number
that predicts renewal, because a contract with 6% adoption doesn't survive a budget review.

Watch the concentration. Three partners at 70% of revenue is a real risk, and it shows up in
diligence as customer concentration whether or not the founder frames it that way.

## Where it goes wrong

- Optimising entirely for the buyer and ending up with a product nobody opens.
- Reporting the consumer count as customers when the contracts are what churn.
- Underpricing because the partner delivers volume, then discovering support costs scale with
  users.
- Having no direct relationship with end users, so you learn about dissatisfaction at renewal.
