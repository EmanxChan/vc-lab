## Why it matters

Redemption gives investors the right to demand their money back after a set period, usually five
to seven years. It exists because funds have finite lives and need a theoretical exit from a
company that is neither failing nor selling.

In practice it's rarely exercised, because a company that could afford to redeem usually has
better things to do with the cash, and one that can't is insolvent. Its real function is
leverage — a reason for the company to engage with an exit conversation.

## What it looks like in practice

Typically structured as redemption at the original price plus accrued dividends, exercisable by
a majority of preferred after a defined period, and often payable in instalments rather than at
once.

The clause matters most for the zombie case: a company doing fine, growing slowly, with no
acquirer and no IPO path. Redemption is the only term that forces that conversation.

## Where it goes wrong

- Ignoring it because it's rarely exercised. It's leverage whether or not it fires.
- Agreeing to redemption at a multiple rather than at cost.
- Not checking how accrued dividends compound into the redemption amount.
- Assuming the company will have the cash. Usually the ones asked don't.
