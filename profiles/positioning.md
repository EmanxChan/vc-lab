## Why it matters

Positioning determines the comparison set, and the comparison set determines whether you win.
It's the highest-leverage thing a company can change without touching the product, and it's
usually the reason a good product isn't selling.

It also cascades. Positioning drives pricing, the ICP, the roadmap, and who you hire. Getting it
wrong misdirects all of them at once.

## What it looks like in practice

The components are the competitive alternative you're replacing, the capability you have that it
doesn't, the value that produces, and the customer who cares most about that value. Those four
settle almost every other marketing question.

Good positioning usually feels narrower than founders want. Narrowing is what makes it work — a
product positioned for everyone is compared against everything.

## Where it goes wrong

- Positioning against the largest market instead of the one you can win.
- Confusing it with messaging. Positioning is the strategy; messaging is the words.
- Positioning on features rather than on the alternative you replace.
- Changing it every quarter, so the market never learns what you are.

## Where the term comes from

Al Ries and Jack Trout's *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* (1981) established the core
idea — that positioning happens in the customer's mind, not in the product. April Dunford's
*Obviously Awesome* (2019) is the modern practitioner's version, and the one most B2B founders
actually use.
