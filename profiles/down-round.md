## Why it matters

A down round is where the protective terms stop being theoretical. Anti-dilution provisions
fire, ownership shifts, and the mechanics that were negotiated years earlier determine who
absorbs the loss.

The second-order effect is usually worse than the arithmetic. Down rounds damage morale, make
options underwater, and signal to the market — which is why companies fight so hard to avoid
them, sometimes by accepting structure that is worse than the lower price would have been.

## What it looks like in practice

The mechanism to understand is the anti-dilution adjustment: full ratchet repricing earlier
investors to the new price, or the far more common weighted average, which adjusts partially
based on how much new money comes in.

Structure is the alternative to a lower headline price, and it is frequently the worse deal. A
flat round with a 2x participating preference can cost common shareholders more than an honest
down round would have.

## Where it goes wrong

- Accepting punitive structure to preserve a headline valuation.
- Not modelling what the anti-dilution adjustment actually does to the cap table.
- Letting options stay deep underwater rather than repricing, and losing the team.
- Treating it as a failure of narrative rather than a repricing that reflects reality.
