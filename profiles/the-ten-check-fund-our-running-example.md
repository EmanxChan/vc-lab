## Why it matters

A worked example is the only way this arithmetic becomes intuitive. Ten cheques, one outcome
that matters, and a set of fees and carry taken along the way — run the numbers once and the
structural constraints of venture become obvious.

The lesson it teaches most clearly is that the fund's return depends almost entirely on the size
of the position in the one company that works, which makes ownership and reserves the decisions
that matter.

## What it looks like in practice

The pattern that emerges from any version of this example: roughly half the positions return
nothing, a few return capital or a small multiple, and one has to return several times the whole
fund for the fund to perform.

That's why a small ownership stake in a great company can be worse for the fund than a large
stake in a good one, and why fund size and cheque size have to be designed together rather than
separately.

## Where it goes wrong

- Building a portfolio model on average outcomes. Averages don't describe power-law
  distributions.
- Assuming ten positions is enough diversification to reliably catch an outlier.
- Ignoring fee drag, which means the portfolio must return well above 1x for LPs to break even.
- Modelling entry ownership without modelling dilution through to exit.
