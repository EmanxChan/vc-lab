## Why it matters

Fiduciary duty is the line between investing your own money and managing someone else's. It is
why a fund has governance documents at all: the LPA, the LPAC, the conflict-of-interest vote and
the reporting cadence are all machinery built to make an abstract duty enforceable.

It also explains a fact that otherwise looks odd — that LPs can force a fund into limited
operations. That is not an investor second-guessing a stock pick. It is the remedy for a breach
of duty.

## What it looks like in practice

The duty shows up as process rather than sentiment. Related-party investments go to the LPAC.
Valuation policy is written down before it is needed, so marks are not set to flatter a raise.
Bad quarters get reported in the same voice as good ones. Allocation between two funds managed by
the same firm follows a stated rule rather than a judgment call.

For anyone writing personal checks, none of this is owed to anybody. That is worth naming
precisely, because it is the single largest difference between angel money and fund money, and
the habits that make a credible fiduciary are cheaper to build before there is a duty than after.

## Where it goes wrong

- Conflicts handled informally because everyone involved is friendly.
- Marks set to support a fundraise rather than to describe the portfolio.
- Fee and expense allocation decided case by case instead of by a written policy.
- Treating transparency as a communications choice rather than an obligation.
- Assuming good intentions are a defence. The duty is procedural, and it is judged on process.
