## Why it matters

Without this definition the liquidation preference would only apply to an actual liquidation,
which is the outcome nobody is planning for. Deeming an acquisition a liquidation is what makes
the preference meaningful in the scenario that actually happens.

The scope of the definition is negotiated and matters. Whether a majority-stake sale, an asset
sale, or an exclusive licence of substantially all IP counts can determine whether preferences
trigger at all.

## What it looks like in practice

Standard definitions cover a merger or consolidation where existing holders no longer control
the survivor, and a sale of substantially all assets. Broader versions catch exclusive licences
and majority stake sales.

It's also the trigger that interacts with vesting acceleration, so the same definition often
decides both who gets paid and whose equity vests.

## Where it goes wrong

- Assuming a preference only applies in bankruptcy. It applies to most exits.
- Leaving asset sales or exclusive licences outside the definition, creating a route around the
  preference.
- Not checking that the definition matches the one used in the vesting acceleration language.
- Treating it as boilerplate when it decides whether the whole preference stack fires.
