## Why it matters

Broad products lose to incumbents because being generally better isn't worth the migration. A
wedge wins because on one specific thing the gap is large enough to overcome switching costs,
and that thing is usually too small for the incumbent to care about.

It's also a discipline for a small team. A wedge tells you what not to build, which is the
harder half of a roadmap at pre-seed.

## What it looks like in practice

A good wedge is acute, frequent, and adjacent to where you eventually want to be. Acute so
people will move. Frequent so you become part of the routine. Adjacent so expansion is natural
rather than a second sale.

The expansion path should already be visible in the wedge itself — the same data, the same
users, the same buyer. If moving from the wedge to the vision requires a new buyer and a new
dataset, it isn't a wedge, it's two companies.

## Where it goes wrong

- Picking a wedge that is genuinely small — a niche with no path out of it.
- Describing the whole vision in the pitch and the wedge nowhere.
- Expanding before the wedge is actually won, and being mediocre at two things.
- Choosing a wedge that doesn't connect to the eventual product, so expansion starts from zero.
