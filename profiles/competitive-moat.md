## Why it matters

It's the difference between a company and a head start. At pre-seed nobody has a moat yet — what
you're underwriting is whether the thing they're building accumulates one as it grows, or stays
equally copyable at every scale.

The question that separates them: what gets harder for a competitor as you get bigger? If the
answer is nothing, growth doesn't buy defensibility, and the company is in a permanent fight it
has to keep winning on execution alone.

## What it looks like in practice

The moats that actually hold are structural: switching costs that rise with usage, network
effects where each user makes it better for the next, proprietary data that compounds,
regulatory positions that took years to earn, or genuine scale economics.

For AI companies this is the live debate. Model quality is a weak moat because it's temporary
and purchasable. Proprietary data flywheels, deep workflow integration, and distribution into
places others can't reach are the durable ones.

## Where it goes wrong

- Claiming a technology moat that a well-funded team could reproduce in two quarters.
- Calling a first-mover advantage a moat. Being early is a position, not a defence.
- Listing patents as a moat without the resources or intent to enforce them.
- Confusing a great team with a moat. Teams are an advantage; they aren't structural.

## Where the term comes from

The term is Warren Buffett's, used through Berkshire's shareholder letters from the 1980s onward
— the image of an economic castle protected by a moat that widens over time. It moved into
venture vocabulary largely intact.
