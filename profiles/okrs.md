## Why it matters

OKRs matter at the point where a company gets big enough that not everyone is in the same
conversation. Before that they're usually overhead — a five-person team that needs OKRs to know
what matters has a different problem.

The value is in what they exclude. Three objectives means everything else is explicitly not this
quarter, and that clarity is the whole benefit.

## What it looks like in practice

Key results have to be outcomes rather than activities. "Ship the new onboarding" is a task and
you can complete it while the metric gets worse. "Activation to 45%" is a result and you can't
fake it.

Google's convention of treating 70% attainment as success exists to keep objectives ambitious.
Teams that hit 100% consistently have learned to sandbag, which quietly defeats the point.

## Where it goes wrong

- Writing key results that are tasks in disguise.
- Setting fifteen of them, which is a to-do list with a framework's name.
- Tying OKRs directly to compensation, which guarantees conservative targets.
- Introducing them at five people, where the overhead exceeds the clarity.

## Where the term comes from

Andy Grove developed the practice at Intel in the 1970s, out of Peter Drucker's management by
objectives. John Doerr brought it to Google in 1999 and later wrote *Measure What Matters* about
it.
