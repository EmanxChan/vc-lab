## Why it matters

Winning 80% of a tiny market beats 2% of a big one, and not just for morale. Concentration makes
word of mouth work, makes references land, makes the product coherent, and makes every sales
conversation shorter than the last.

Founders resist it because it sounds like limiting ambition, and because a small first market
reads badly in a deck. The good version of the deck shows the beachhead and the sequence out of
it.

## What it looks like in practice

A beachhead needs three things: the customers talk to each other, they share enough of the same
problem that one product serves all of them, and it's adjacent to a bigger market you can move
into with the same product.

The test of whether you've won it is whether new customers arrive already knowing who you are.
That's when the beachhead has done its job and the next segment becomes cheaper to enter than
the first one was.

## Where it goes wrong

- Choosing a beachhead with no adjacency, so success there teaches you nothing about the next
  market.
- Leaving too early, before the reference effects that make it worth doing have kicked in.
- Picking a segment because it's easy rather than because it's connected.
- Defining it so broadly that it isn't a beachhead, it's a market.

## Where the term comes from

The framing is Geoffrey Moore's in *Crossing the Chasm* (1991) — the D-Day analogy of taking one
beach completely before moving inland — later systematised by Bill Aulet at MIT as the first
step of *Disciplined Entrepreneurship*.
