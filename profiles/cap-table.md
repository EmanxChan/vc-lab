## Why it matters

The cap table records ownership, and people read it as a payout schedule. It isn't — the
preference stack and the waterfall decide what anyone actually receives, and in a modest exit
those can diverge from the percentages entirely.

A clean cap table is also a practical asset. Messy ones — dozens of small holders, undocumented
promises, unassigned IP, missing 83(b) filings — slow rounds down and occasionally kill them.

## What it looks like in practice

The useful version is fully diluted: all issued shares, all options granted and unallocated, all
warrants, and all convertible instruments modelled at conversion. Anything less understates
dilution.

It should be maintained continuously rather than reconstructed at each round. Companies that
rebuild the cap table under time pressure during diligence are the ones that discover the
problems at the worst moment.

## Where it goes wrong

- Reading percentages as a payout. The waterfall is what pays.
- Keeping it on a spreadsheet that only one person updates.
- Excluding unissued option pool and outstanding SAFEs from the fully diluted count.
- Letting verbal equity promises accumulate outside the document.
