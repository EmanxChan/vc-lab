## Why it matters

D2C trades distribution cost for acquisition cost, and whether that's a good trade depends
entirely on the margin structure. You keep the retailer's cut, but you inherit their job —
finding customers — and unlike shelf space, paid acquisition gets more expensive as you scale.

The 2010s D2C boom and bust was largely this arithmetic catching up. Cheap social ads made the
model look structurally sound; when acquisition costs rose, businesses with 40% gross margins
discovered they had no room.

## What it looks like in practice

The businesses that hold up have high gross margin, real repeat purchase, or both. Margin buys
room to acquire; repeat purchase means you only pay acquisition once. A product with 70% margin
bought four times a year is a different business from one with 35% margin bought once.

The direct relationship is the real prize when it's used — first-party data, faster product
iteration, and the ability to launch adjacent products to an audience you already own.

## Where it goes wrong

- Building on a single acquisition channel whose price you don't control.
- Confusing first-order profitability with a business — most D2C only works on repeat.
- Ignoring returns, shipping, and payment processing when calculating margin.
- Scaling ad spend before knowing the payback period.
