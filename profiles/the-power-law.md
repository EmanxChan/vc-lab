## Why it matters

Venture returns follow a power law: a very small number of investments produce most of the
return, and the median investment returns little or nothing. This isn't a risk to be managed —
it's the structure of the asset class.

Everything else in fund construction follows from it. Portfolio size, ownership targets, reserve
policy, and the willingness to lose money on most positions are all consequences of this one
fact.

## What it looks like in practice

The practical implications are uncomfortable. You need enough positions to have a reasonable
chance of catching an outlier, enough ownership in that outlier for it to matter, and the
discipline to keep funding the winner rather than rescuing the losers.

It also means average outcomes are the wrong thing to model. A portfolio built to produce a
reliable 2x will not produce a fund-returning outcome, because the strategies that avoid zeros
also avoid the tail.

## Where it goes wrong

- Building a portfolio model on expected averages rather than on the tail.
- Diversifying so broadly that no position can return the fund.
- Concentrating so narrowly that missing the outlier is likely.
- Deploying reserves to defend failing positions rather than to double down on the working one.
