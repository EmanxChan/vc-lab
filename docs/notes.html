Sprint 1 — Introduction to VC Basics

1.1 — Overview of the Venture Capital Industry

vcl.to/VI1-1

Venture capital is a specialized form of private equity: capital from a fund invested into companies with significant growth potential. VCs fund the innovative startups that traditional lenders avoid, accepting substantial risk in exchange for equity.

The industry runs on three stages — fundraise (GPs raise from LPs), invest (deploy into startups for equity), exit (acquisition or IPO). Firms make money two ways: ~2% annual management fees and ~20% carried interest.

Roles inside a firm: Managing Partners (strategy and LP relationships), General Partners (deal decisions, portfolio guidance), Venture Partners (sector expertise), and Associates/Analysts (diligence and research).

The pitch to founders isn't the money — it's mentorship, connections, recruiting help, and exit planning. Capital is the commodity.

1.2 — Role of Venture Capital in Funding Startups

vcl.to/VI1-2

VC compounds beyond the individual company. Funded startups create unicorns, unicorns create jobs and wealth, and that wealth recycles into the ecosystem as new founders and new angels. A thriving startup community attracts talent, which attracts more investment.

The societal argument: venture-backed companies address real problems in transportation, healthcare, and food access — improving accessibility, efficiency, and quality of life while disrupting incumbents.

1.3 — The Investment Process and Timeline

vcl.to/VI1-3

The process is a funnel, not a pipeline: roughly 500 leads → 1–2 investments.

  • Sourcing — networking, events, platforms, accelerators, incubators
  • Due diligence — business model, market, competition, financials, team
  • Term sheet — non-binding; amount, valuation, ownership, rights, preferences
  • Documentation & close — investment agreements, shareholder contracts, wire
  • Post-investment support — board seat, network access, help scaling
  • Follow-on & exit — later rounds, then IPO / M&A / secondary
Conviction isn't the hard part. Volume at the top of the funnel is what makes conviction possible.

Sprint 2 — Venture Capital Firm Structures

2.1 — Venture Capital Structures

vcl.to/VI2-1

Four ways to organize venture capital:

  • Traditional funds — collect from partners, invest across many startups, plan for exits
  • Venture builders — create and manage companies internally, growing businesses from within
  • Outsourced funds — platforms letting part-time or first-time investors run a fund
  • SPVs — simplified vehicles pooling capital for a single specific deal

2.2 — Venture Firm Entities

vcl.to/VI2-2

Three entities, three jobs. What people call "a VC firm" is actually all three:

EntityJobPaid by
Management Company (ManCo)Owns brand & IP, employs staff, runs vendors and opsManagement fee
General Partner (GP)Makes investment decisions, oversees the fund(s)Carried interest
The FundCollects LP capital, holds securities, calls capital— it's the vehicle

All three are typically pass-through entities — no tax at the entity level, income flows to individual returns, avoiding double taxation.

The separation exists so fee income, carry, and investor capital never sit in the same pocket. It's structural discipline, not paperwork. → See the capital flow diagram

2.3 — Venture Capital Processes

vcl.to/VI2-3

Create the fund

  • 1. Write the thesis — geography, sectors, strategy, stage, target size, name (4–6 weeks)
  • 2. Form the team — partners known 5+ years; test commitment before offers
  • 3. Pitch LPs — define archetypes, secure PACT commitment letters
  • 4. Complete fund formation — at 10% or $1M committed: counsel, domicile, vendors, entities, capital calls

Make an investment

  • 5. Close a deal — source to thesis, build conviction, investment committee, diligence, negotiate, sign, wire, set up updates

Return the money

  • 6. Distribute proceeds — collect exit proceeds, determine carry status, calculate per partner accounts, net fees, check side letters, communicate, distribute on schedule

Sprint 3 — Venture Capital Performance

3.1 — The Power Law in VC

vcl.to/VI3-1

One investment returns more than all the others combined — often by orders of magnitude. Rooted in Pareto's distribution work.

  • Portfolios are built expecting most companies to fail or flatline
  • The job isn't avoiding losers — it's guaranteeing exposure to the one winner
  • Drives the hunt for "Blue Ocean Deals" — untapped markets, strong early adoption
  • Fund size and check size must be set so one outlier can move the whole fund
  • It's why access to competitive deals is fought over so hard
VC is not a diversification model wearing a risk costume. It's a concentrated bet on transformational companies, with diversification used only to buy more tickets in the same lottery.

3.2 — Venture Fund Economics

vcl.to/VI3-2
  • Management fee — ~2%/yr of LP commitments. A $50M fund → $1M/yr. Covers salaries, rent, insurance, diligence, travel.
  • Carried interest — 20% of profits. $20M profit → $4M GP / $16M LP.
  • Hurdle rate — carry applies only above a preferred return (~8%), so LPs are made whole first.
  • Origin — post-WWII, standardized by figures like Tom Perkins, who believed managers should earn significantly from profits they generated.

3.3 — Venture Fund Metrics

vcl.to/VI3-3
MetricWhat it answersWatch out for
MOICWhat multiple on money in?Ignores time entirely
TVPITotal value (realized + paper) / paid inBlends real and imaginary
DPIHow much cash actually came back?The honest one
RVPIHow much is still in the ground?Paper marks, self-reported
IRRAnnualized, time-weighted returnFlattered by early markups; gameable by timing
TVPI shows the size of the outcome, DPI shows how much of it is real, IRR shows how fast you got there. Never read IRR alone.

Sprint 4 — Finding and Closing Limited Partners

4.1 — The Importance of Limited Partners

govclab.com/2023/08/16/the-importance-of-limited-partners/

Without LPs, venture capital would not exist. They supply the capital and hold managers to fiduciary duty. But good LPs do more than fund — they shape strategy through advisory committees and feedback, and they show up for the community.

Quality over size: cultivate a diverse LP base with no single investor above ~20%, avoid problematic side letters, and screen for trustworthiness, transparency, and long-term vision. Impatient or opaque LPs can sink a fund.

4.2 — Limited Partner Archetypes

govclab.com/2023/08/16/lp-archetypes/

Common archetypes: high-net-worth individuals ($1M+ liquid, clustered among professionals, executives, entrepreneurs, and investors), family offices, funds of funds, pension funds, sovereign funds, insurance companies, and corporate/strategic investors.

Motivation differs by type. A corporate LP may invest for strategic visibility into emerging technology rather than pure return — which changes what they want from you.

4.3 — Sourcing and Closing Limited Partners

vcl.to/VI4-3

An LP construction model for a $10M fund:

Check sizeCommitmentsTypical archetype
$1M2Family offices, funds of funds, corporate VC
$500K6Exited founders, tech executives
$250K12HNWIs, bankers, larger VC firms
$100K20Friends, alumni networks, angels

Initial closes typically capture 10–25% of target fund size. The eleven-step funnel: source → connector intro → initial meeting → pitch → materials → PACT signed → diligence → legal review → agreement signed → capital call.

"Identifying real LPs requires searching with a combination of less obvious keywords" — the obvious searches surface the same over-solicited names everyone else found.

4.4 — Maintaining Relationships with LPs

govclab.com/2023/08/16/maintaining-lp-relationships/

The goal is an enduring personal and financial relationship, so LPs re-up in Fund II. Communication should be transparent (wins and setbacks), matter of fact (no embellishment), data rich, and clear (no jargon).

Touchpoints: email updates, scheduled calls, monthly newsletters, quarterly or annual reports, and in-person events. Cadence follows the sector — quarterly for fast-moving tech and early stage, annual for longer cycles like biotech.

Avoid surprises, especially about exits. Success signals: prompt responses, timely capital calls, and reinvestment.
