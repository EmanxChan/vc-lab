Sprint 2.3: Venture Capital ProcessesJuly 2026

Submitted: July 2026 Prompt: Understand the VC investment process from sourcing to exit. Pick the step you're least familiar with, research it, and write a paragraph plus five links. Source: https://vcl.to/VI2-3 → https://govclab.com/2023/08/02/venture-capital-processes/

Step researched: Distributing Proceeds

The step I was least familiar with is the final one — distributing proceeds. I understood how a fund raises money and writes checks, but not how cash actually gets back to LPs. What I learned is that it runs through a "distribution waterfall," a strict order of payment. First, LPs get their invested capital returned. Second, they receive a preferred return (a hurdle rate, industry standard around 8%). Only after both are satisfied does the GP earn carried interest — typically a 20% share of profits, with the remaining 80% going to LPs. The big structural choice is European vs. American waterfall: a European waterfall pays carry only after the entire fund has returned all LP capital, while an American waterfall pays carry deal-by-deal as each exit lands. European is LP-friendly and is the norm for emerging managers; American gets the GP paid sooner but risks clawback if later deals fail. I also learned the operational side matters more than I assumed — distributions require calculating each partner's account, netting out fees, checking side letters, and communicating clearly with LPs on a set timeline. Doing that cleanly is a visible signal of whether a GP is professional or amateur, and it directly shapes whether LPs re-up in Fund II.

Sprint 3: Fund Economics & PerformanceJuly 2026

Submitted: July 2026 Prompt: Understand how VC funds make money and measure performance. Power Law, MOIC/TVPI/IRR, "2 and 20," and whether your target market and stage produce outlier outcomes. Sources: vcl.to/VI3-1 · vcl.to/VI3-2 · vcl.to/VI3-3

The Power Law

Venture returns aren't spread evenly across a portfolio — one investment typically returns more than every other investment combined. That single fact drives everything about how a fund is built: you construct a portfolio expecting most companies to fail or go sideways, so the job isn't picking safe winners, it's making sure you're in the one company capable of returning the whole fund. It also means fund size and check size have to be set so that a single outlier can actually move the fund's numbers.

MOIC, TVPI, and IRR

  • MOIC (Multiple on Invested Capital) — the raw multiple on what was put in. Simple, but ignores time.
  • TVPI (Total Value to Paid-In) — distributions plus remaining portfolio value, divided by capital paid in. The headline "how is the fund doing" number, blending realized and unrealized. Splits into DPI (cash actually returned) and RVPI (paper value still in the ground).
  • IRR — the annualized, time-weighted rate of return. It's the one that lets LPs compare a VC fund against other asset classes, but it flatters early markups and can be gamed by timing.

Together: TVPI tells you the size of the outcome, DPI tells you how much is real, IRR tells you how fast you got there.

"2 and 20" — alignment and tension

The GP charges roughly 2% of committed capital annually to run the firm — salaries, diligence, travel — and takes 20% of profits as carried interest, usually only after LPs get their capital back plus a preferred return (~8%). The alignment is real: carry means the GP only gets meaningfully rich if the LPs do. The tension is that management fees are paid regardless of performance, so a large fund can pay its GPs well even if it never returns capital — which is why LPs scrutinize fund size, and why carry (not fees) is what a serious emerging manager points to.

My market and stage: do they produce outliers?

My focus is AI and humanity at pre-seed to seed in the United States, and I think the honest answer is yes on stage and "yes, with a caveat" on market. Pre-seed and seed is precisely where power-law economics work in a fund's favor: entry ownership is cheap, so a single company reaching a large outcome can return the entire fund, which is much harder to achieve when you're paying Series A prices for de-risked companies. AI is also the clearest platform shift of this decade, and the US is where the largest outcomes in that shift are being priced. The caveat is my "humanity" filter. Companies applying AI to health, education, labor, and civic life often face regulated buyers, longer sales cycles, and acquirer sets that top out in the hundreds of millions rather than the tens of billions — so the distribution of outcomes may be wider and shallower than in pure infrastructure or developer tooling. What that means for my approach is that fund size is the variable I have to get right, not thesis conviction: I should size the fund small enough that a $300–500M exit still returns it, target meaningful ownership at entry rather than spraying small checks, and hold real reserves so I can keep buying into the one company that does break out. I'd rather run a fund where several good outcomes produce a strong return than one that quietly requires a decacorn to work at all.

Fund Language SetupJuly 2026

Submitted: July 2026 Prompt: Write a 12-term "VC language I will use" cheat sheet, then the same terms with a "why it matters" line, then 6 practice prompts, then a plain-English script.

Part 1 — Cheat sheet

  1. Thesis: The specific slice of the market a fund invests in — sector, stage, and geography.
  2. Pre-seed: The first institutional money in, usually before meaningful revenue.
  3. SAFE: A simple agreement that converts to equity at the next priced round.
  4. Post-money valuation cap: The ceiling price at which a SAFE converts into equity.
  5. Check size: The dollar amount a fund invests in a single company.
  6. Ownership target: The percentage of a company a fund aims to hold after its investment.
  7. Reserves: Capital held back at the start to invest again in companies that are working.
  8. Pro rata: The right to invest in later rounds to maintain your ownership percentage.
  9. Power Law: The pattern where one investment returns more than all the others combined.
  10. Fund size: Total capital committed by LPs, which determines what exit size actually matters.
  11. 2 and 20: A 2% annual management fee to run the firm, plus 20% of profits as carried interest.
  12. DPI: Distributed to Paid-In — the cash actually returned to investors, not paper value.

Part 2 — Same terms, with why it matters

  1. Thesis: The specific slice of the market a fund invests in — sector, stage, and geography. Why it matters: It's the first question every LP asks, and a vague answer reads as an unformed fund.
  2. Pre-seed: The first institutional money in, usually before meaningful revenue. Why it matters: It sets what diligence is even possible — at this stage you're underwriting the founder, not the numbers.
  3. SAFE: A simple agreement that converts to equity at the next priced round. Why it matters: Most pre-seed checks go in on SAFEs, so if you can't discuss one fluently you can't discuss the deal.
  4. Post-money valuation cap: The ceiling price at which a SAFE converts into equity. Why it matters: It's the number that actually determines your ownership, and founders often quote it loosely.
  5. Check size: The dollar amount a fund invests in a single company. Why it matters: Founders use it to decide whether you can lead or only follow.
  6. Ownership target: The percentage of a company a fund aims to hold after its investment. Why it matters: It's the discipline that connects your check size to your fund's math — without it you're guessing.
  7. Reserves: Capital held back at the start to invest again in companies that are working. Why it matters: No reserves means you get diluted out of your winner exactly when it's proving itself.
  8. Pro rata: The right to invest in later rounds to maintain your ownership percentage. Why it matters: It's the mechanism that lets a small early check keep mattering in a breakout.
  9. Power Law: The pattern where one investment returns more than all the others combined. Why it matters: It explains nearly every investor behavior founders find confusing, including pressure to go bigger.
  10. Fund size: Total capital committed by LPs, which determines what exit size actually matters. Why it matters: A $40M exit is a win for a $10M fund and a rounding error for a $200M one.
  11. 2 and 20: A 2% annual management fee to run the firm, plus 20% of profits as carried interest. Why it matters: Fees are paid regardless of performance, so pointing to carry signals you're serious about returns.
  12. DPI: Distributed to Paid-In — the cash actually returned to investors, not paper value. Why it matters: Anyone can show a good TVPI from markups; DPI is the number that can't be argued with.

Part 3 — Six practice prompts

  1. (founder) "What are you optimizing for on this round — the cap, or getting the right people on the cap table?"
  2. (founder) "What does the next 18 months buy you, and what has to be true for a Series A to be obvious?"
  3. (founder) "Are you raising on a SAFE or pricing this round? What cap are you anchoring to and why that number?"
  4. (LP) "When you look at emerging managers, are you weighting DPI from prior angel investments, or is it mostly thesis and access?"
  5. (LP) "How do you think about fund size for a pre-seed manager — what's the ceiling where the math stops working for you?"
  6. (either) "How much of the round is reserved for follow-on, and who's expected to lead the next one?"

Part 4 — My venture vocabulary in plain English

I'm early in venture and I'd rather be precise than perform fluency. Here's how I think about it in plain terms: a fund is a pool of money from investors called LPs, and my job is to decide which startups it goes into and to make sure enough of it comes back. Venture returns follow a power law — one company usually returns more than everything else combined — so the discipline is less about avoiding bad companies than about making sure I own enough of the one that works, which is why things like ownership targets and reserves matter more than they sound like they should. I focus on AI and humanity at pre-seed to seed in the US, which means I'm usually underwriting a founder rather than a spreadsheet. I'm learning the mechanics quickly and in public — I keep a working glossary and notes at https://vc-lab.vercel.app — and I'd rather ask a direct question than nod along to a term I only half understand.

Your Moat WedgeJuly 2026

Submitted: July 2026 Prompt: Write 5 venture wedge statements for assistant-AI / AI co-working use cases, name the exact founder harm each prevents, pick the top 2 and identify the buyer, then write "Wedge Ready."

Part 1 — Five wedge statements

  1. We back AI co-workers that run back-office operations for small clinics at pre-seed in the US to prevent owner-operators from losing their best hours to insurance paperwork.
  1. We back AI agents that capture institutional knowledge as people leave at pre-seed in the US to prevent founders from re-buying knowledge they already paid for.
  1. We back AI co-working tools for deskless and frontline teams at pre-seed in the US to prevent operators from losing good staff to scheduling and communication chaos.
  1. We back AI that drafts and files regulated documentation at pre-seed in the US to prevent founders from betting the company on a filing a junior hire prepared at midnight.
  1. We back AI operations partners for teams under ten people at pre-seed in the US to prevent founders from hiring a role they can't afford eight months early.

Part 2 — The exact harm prevented

  1. Clinic back-office. The owner spends 12–15 hours a week on claims and prior authorizations. None of it is billable. Denied claims go uncontested, so earned revenue gets written off.
  1. Knowledge capture. A senior departure costs 3–6 months of replacement ramp. The remaining staff absorb the gap, get overloaded, and become the next people to quit.
  1. Frontline co-working. Missed shifts and lost messages drive turnover in roles that are expensive to refill, and it burns out the managers who then leave too.
  1. Regulated documentation. One bad filing means fines, remediation cost, and a stalled launch or dead deal. That's a cash and survival event, not an inconvenience.
  1. AI ops partner. The founder either burns runway on a salary revenue can't support, or skips the hire and personally becomes the bottleneck on every process in the company.

Part 3 — Top two wedges

AI operations partner for teams under ten people The buyer is the founder or first ops hire at a 3–10 person company with revenue but no operational headcount. They pay early because the alternative is a $90K salary they can't justify yet, and a few hundred dollars a month against that comparison is an easy yes.

AI back-office for small clinics The buyer is the owner-operator of an independent practice or their practice manager. They pay early because recovered claims and reclaimed clinical hours are countable in the first month, and this buyer already pays per seat for billing software.

Part 4 — Wedge Ready

  • Top wedge: AI operations partners for teams under ten people
  • Target geo: United States
  • Stage: Pre-seed to Seed
  • Founder harm prevented: Founders either burn runway on an ops hire the revenue can't support, or skip it and become the bottleneck themselves. Both cost months and cash they don't have.
