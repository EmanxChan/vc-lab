### SET 1 — Understanding Dealflow

**Copy from here:**

The six steps of dealflow management, in order, are sourcing, filtering, diligence, decisions,
closing, and followup. Reading the titles in sequence was itself the useful part — I had been
treating dealflow as one activity called "finding deals," when really only the first sixth of it is
finding anything.

The step I am least familiar with is filtering, specifically the split between first-level and
second-level filters. My situation is that I have inflow and no filter. I am an alum of Plug and
Play, Grid110, and Material Change, so introductions reach me without my doing much, and until now
I have handled each one ad hoc — a call, a good conversation, no decision. What I want to develop
is a written screen I apply the same way every time, so that a pass is a repeatable decision
rather than a mood, and so I can tell a founder no in a way that is fast, specific, and does not
cost me the relationship.

What I learned. A first-level filter is a mechanical screen against the thesis itself — sector,
stage, geography, and price range — and it is supposed to be cheap enough to automate, because its
only job is to clear obvious mismatches before they cost anyone a meeting. A second-level filter is
where human judgment starts: a focused call, then a quick look at market size, customer validation,
and team depth. The idea that changed how I will actually work is that the second-level filter is
where you spend relationship capital rather than save it. The Decile article's example is an
investor who passed on a company with under 50% customer retention but introduced the founder to a
retention mentor on the way out. That is a pass that leaves the founder better off, and it is the
version of "no" I want to be known for, because founder-first is the whole basis of my dealflow.
Reading VC Lab's own investment process article alongside it put a number on why the filter has to
be cheap: roughly 500 leads produce one or two investments, so almost everything you do in this job
is saying no, and the cost of each no compounds. Bessemer's Anti-Portfolio, which is a public list
of the great companies that firm passed on, is the counterweight — a filter tight enough to never
waste a meeting is also tight enough to screen out the outlier, and the honest answer is that both
failure modes are real and you are choosing which one to risk.

Where I want to go deeper later in the program. Two places. First, decisions — how conviction
actually gets formed and written down at pre-seed, when there is no revenue to analyze and the
whole underwriting is the founder. Second, followup, which I suspect is the step that separates
angels who see the next deal from angels who see one deal. Both matter more to me than closing
mechanics right now, because as an angel writing personal checks I am not negotiating complex terms
yet, but I am absolutely living or dying on whether founders come back.

Sources I used:
1. https://decilegroup.com/articles/deals-filtering-emerging-vc — first- and second-level filters
2. https://decilegroup.com/articles/deals-sourcing-emerging-vc — where deals actually come from
3. https://govclab.com/2023/06/14/vc-investment-process/ — the 500-to-1 funnel ratio
4. https://www.bvp.com/anti-portfolio — the cost of a filter that is too tight
5. https://www.ycombinator.com/library — founder-side view of how screens read from the outside

### SET 4 — Deal Memo

**Same constraint.** The memo has to be grounded in a real founder conversation, and the peer
feedback has to be given to real peers. Neither is mine to invent. Two things I can hand you: the
interview that produces a memo worth writing, and the closing reflection, which is genuinely

**The founder interview — questions mapped to your eight-section memo template.**

Your template already lives in `sprints/sprint-5-venture-deals.md`. These are the questions that
fill it, in order, so the memo writes itself afterward:

1. The one-liner — "In your words, what do you do, and who is it for?" Write down their phrasing
   verbatim, not your cleaned-up version.
2. Why now — "What changed in the last two years that makes this possible when it wasn't before?"
3. Team — "Why are you the people who solve this? What did you do before that makes this obvious?"
4. Market — "Who writes the check today for the thing you're replacing, and what do they pay?"
5. Thesis fit — "How do you know when the product is working for a user? What signal do you watch?"
   For a trust product, the honest answer to this is the whole investment.
6. The bet — "What has to be true in 18 months for the next round to be easy?"
7. Terms — "What are you raising, on what instrument, at what cap, and how much is committed?"
8. Anti-memo — "What's the strongest argument against this company that you'd make yourself?" The
   founders who answer this well are the ones worth backing.

Ask number 8 last and let the silence sit. It is the highest-signal question on the list.

**Feedback on two peers' memos.** The set says to focus on the evaluation rather than the quality
of the deal. Concretely, that means asking of each memo: is there a claim in here that is asserted
rather than evidenced? Is there a stated bet, or does the memo only say why the company is good?
Does the anti-memo argue honestly, or is it a straw man the author already knew how to knock down?
Those three questions produce useful feedback on any memo regardless of the company.

**Copy from here — where AI helped and where humans must be involved:**

AI did the compression. It read everything I gave it — the deck, the transcript, the public
material — and produced a structured draft in ten minutes that would have taken me most of a day,

Where humans have to stay in it is judgment about people, and the reason is structural rather than
