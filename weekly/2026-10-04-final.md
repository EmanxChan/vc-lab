## SET 1 — Understanding Dealflow

_Editor index 0. Paste as-is. Unchanged this week._

The six steps of dealflow management, in the order the articles lay them out, are sourcing, filtering, diligence, decisions, closing, and follow-up: How VCs Source Deals in Emerging VC, How VCs Filter Dealflow in Emerging VC, Early Stage Diligence in Emerging VC, Decision-making in Emerging VC, Closing Deals in Emerging VC, and How VCs Manage Followup. Reading the titles in sequence was the useful part on its own. Dealflow is the stream of startups arriving at an investor, and I had been treating the whole thing as one activity called finding deals, when only the first sixth of it is finding anything.

The aspect I am least familiar with is filtering, and specifically the split between the first-level and the second-level filter. My honest situation is that I have inflow and no filter. I am an alum of Plug and Play, Grid110, and Material Change, so introductions reach me without much effort on my part, and until now I have handled each one ad hoc: a call, a good conversation, no decision. What I want to develop is a written screen I apply the same way every time, so that a pass is a repeatable decision rather than a mood, and so I can say no quickly and specifically without spending the relationship.

What I learned. A first-level filter is a mechanical screen against the thesis itself, on sector, geography, stage, and valuation range, and it is supposed to be cheap, because its only job is to clear obvious mismatches before they cost anyone a meeting. A second-level filter is where human judgment starts: a focused first call of about thirty minutes, then pre-diligence on market size, customer validation, scalability, and team depth. Three things changed how I will actually work. First, a pass is where you spend relationship capital rather than save it. The Decile article's example is an investor who passes on a company with customer retention under 50% and introduces the founder to a mentor who specializes in churn on the way out, which is the version of no I want to be known for. Second, the scale of it. VC Lab's investment process article puts numbers on the funnel: roughly 500 leads become 100 potential investments, then 50 meetings, 20 in-depth reviews, 10 due diligence processes, 5 term sheets, and 1 investment. Almost all of this job is saying no, so the cost of each no compounds, and Bessemer's Anti-Portfolio, the public list of great companies that firm passed on, is the counterweight: a filter tight enough never to waste a meeting is also tight enough to screen out the outlier. Third, and this is the one I did not expect, the article's closing example is a VC who realizes after twenty second-stage calls that her strongest filter is not financial at all, and that she is really testing for coachability and founder-solution fit. At pre-seed there is no revenue to analyze, so that is not a refinement of the screen, it is the screen. The same article makes the point that a thesis stated in four words turns everyone you meet into a deal scout, which lands for me specifically: if I cannot say my filter in one breath, the three alumni networks I am counting on cannot route deals to me.

Since writing that I built the filter and ran it. I screened the 29 published companies from my own accelerator's three Seattle batches against four questions: is it AI, does it touch trust between people, is it pre-seed, is it in the United States. One company survived to diligence. The exercise taught me something the articles did not. The first-level filter was easy and fast, exactly as described, but almost every hard call was a company that passed all four questions and still did not belong, which is a second-level problem the articles frame as being about founder quality and traction. For me it turned out to be about the thesis itself not being precise enough to reject a near miss, so I had to write down what looks like a fit and is not before the filter would work at all.

Later in the program I want to go deeper on two of the six steps. Decisions, because I want to know how conviction actually gets formed and written down at pre-seed, when the underwriting is almost entirely the founder. And follow-up, which I suspect is the step that separates angels who see the next deal from angels who see one deal. Closing mechanics matter less to me right now, because as an angel writing personal checks I am not negotiating complicated terms yet, but I do live or die on whether founders come back.

Plug and Play is one of the three ecosystems I came up through, so this was my claimed dealflow edge tested against a real list rather than asserted on a slide. Twenty-seven were passes, one is worth tracking, and one went into diligence.

The passes did more work than the hit did. Writing 27 reasons in a row made me name the four ways a company can look like this thesis and not be it: trust in machines rather than trust between people, AI as a method rather than as the substance, efficiency dressed up as connection, and AI that spends trust rather than building it. That last group is the one that proves the filter works, because those companies are AI, United States, pre-seed and human-facing, and still wrong. The test I came out of the screen with is one line, and I now apply it before anything else: does this change how two people regard each other, or only how one party regards a machine?
