Two terms I genuinely did not understand before this sprint:

1. Seniority. Later rounds usually get paid before earlier ones when a company sells. Being first
in is not being first paid. This one reorganized how I read a cap table. When Kabbage sold, Series
F took $161M first, then Series E took $166M, then D took $50M, then C took $31M. Only $22M was
left for Series A and B — even though common shareholders still held roughly 28% of the company. I
had assumed early investors were protected by being early. They are not; they are last in line
among the preferred.

2. Capital call. The fund asking its investors to actually send money they have already committed.
Capital is drawn down over years, not paid upfront. An investor commits $500K; in year one the fund
calls $100K and the other $400K stays in that investor's account until it is needed. Once I
understood this, the notice period in a partnership agreement — usually 15 days — stopped looking
like boilerplate and started looking like the thing that determines whether a fund can actually
close a deal on time.

## SET 3 — VC Structures & Entities

**Prompt (verbatim):** Study venture fund structures and entities. Describe Limited Partner,
Management Company, Fund entity, and General Partner roles in your own words. Create a diagram
showing entity interactions and capital flow. Provide the diagram's public URL and explain the
"ManCo, GP, LP structure" briefly.

**Copy from here:**

What people call "a VC firm" is actually three or four separate legal entities with deliberately
different jobs. Here is each one in my own words.

Limited Partner (LP). The investor in the fund — family offices, funds of funds, pension funds,
exited founders, tech executives, and high-net-worth individuals. LPs commit capital and have it
called over time as the fund needs it. They do not pick companies and they do not run anything.
"Limited" refers to their liability, not their influence: they can lose what they committed and no
more.

Management Company (ManCo). The operating business. It owns the firm's brand and intellectual
property, employs the staff, signs the vendor contracts, and pays the bills. It is funded by the
management fee, roughly 2% of committed capital per year. This is the entity you would actually go
to work at.

The Fund. A limited partnership that exists to hold money and shares. LPs commit to it, it calls
their capital, it buys equity in startups, and it holds those securities on everyone's behalf. It
has no employees. Most firms run several funds at once, each one a separate vehicle with its own
vintage year and its own set of LPs.

General Partner (GP). The decision-making entity, made up of managing partners, general partners,
and venture partners. The GP chooses every investment the fund makes and carries unlimited
liability for those decisions. In exchange it earns carried interest — typically 20% of profits,
and only after the LPs have received their capital back plus a preferred return of around 8%.

Diagram — how capital flows through a venture fund. Public URL:
https://vc-lab.vercel.app/structure.html

The same page is mirrored at https://emanxchan.github.io/vc-lab/structure.html. It traces the full
loop in six steps: (1) LPs commit capital and it is called over time into the Fund; (2) the Fund
pays roughly a 2% annual management fee across to the Management Company; (3) the Fund invests in
portfolio companies; (4) the General Partner is the entity deciding which companies those are; (5)
exit proceeds come back into the Fund through an IPO, an acquisition, or a secondary sale; and (6)
the Fund distributes — first returning LP capital, then the preferred return of about 8%, then
splitting the remainder 80% to LPs and 20% to the GP as carry.

Briefly, on what "ManCo, GP, LP structure" means. The phrase describes the three-entity setup that
nearly every venture firm uses. What looks from the outside like one company is three entities with
separated jobs: the ManCo runs the business and is paid fees, the GP makes investment decisions and
is paid carry, and the Fund holds the LPs' capital and the securities it buys.

The separation is the point, and it took me a while to see that. It keeps operating expenses,
performance compensation, and investor capital in three different pockets, so a firm cannot quietly
fund payroll out of investor money, and one fund's problems cannot reach another fund's assets. It
also isolates liability — the GP carries unlimited liability for investment decisions while LPs
risk only what they committed. All three are typically pass-through entities, so profits are not
taxed at the entity level and instead flow through to individual returns, which avoids double
taxation on the same gains. Practically, it means one person can wear three hats at once: operator
of the ManCo, decision-maker at the GP, and fiduciary to the Fund. It is structural discipline, not
paperwork.

**Article draft — copy from here into LinkedIn:**

Title: A venture fund is three companies wearing one name

I am partway through VC Lab's Venture Institute, and the thing that has most changed how I read
this industry is not a valuation trick or a sourcing tactic. It is that a venture capital firm is
not one company. It is three, and once you see the seams you cannot unsee them.

From the outside, a firm has a name, a logo, and a website. Inside, there are three separate legal
entities doing deliberately different jobs.

The Management Company is the business. It owns the brand, employs the people, signs the leases,
and pays for the coffee. It runs on a management fee of roughly 2% of committed capital a year.

The Fund is a limited partnership that holds money and shares. It has no employees. It exists to
collect capital from investors, buy equity in startups, and hold those shares on everyone's behalf.
Most firms are running several of these at once.

The General Partner is the decision-making entity. It picks every investment and carries unlimited
liability for those picks. It gets paid in carried interest — typically 20% of the profits, and
only after investors have their money back plus a preferred return of around 8%.

Why should anyone outside venture care? Because the separation is not an accounting quirk. It is
the mechanism that keeps three kinds of money in three different pockets: operating expenses,
performance pay, and other people's capital. A firm cannot quietly fund payroll out of investor
money. One fund's bad year cannot reach another fund's assets. If you have ever wondered why a
firm's partners can be personally on the hook for a decision while its investors are not, this is
where that lives.

The part I found genuinely surprising is what happens at the end, when a company sells and the cash
comes back. It does not get split by ownership percentage. It runs through a distribution
waterfall, which is a strict order of payment. First, investors get their invested capital
returned. Second, they get a preferred return, an industry-standard hurdle of around 8%. Only after
both of those are satisfied does the firm earn its 20% share.

There is a real fork in the road inside that. A European waterfall pays the firm its share only
after the entire fund has returned all investor capital. An American waterfall pays deal by deal as
each exit lands. European is investor-friendly and is the norm for emerging managers; American gets
the firm paid sooner but risks a clawback if later deals go badly.

Here is the translation for people outside venture, which is most of the people I talk to. Being
early does not mean being paid first. Later investors are usually paid before earlier ones, and
everyone holding preferred stock is paid before everyone holding common stock — which is what
employees and founders hold. When Kabbage sold, the later rounds took the money in order from the
top: $161M, then $166M, then $50M, then $31M. Only $22M was left for the earliest investors, even
though common shareholders still held roughly 28% of the company.

If you have equity in a startup, that ordering matters more to your outcome than your percentage
does. A headline sale price and your slice of the cap table are two different questions, and the
waterfall is the thing standing between them. I did not understand that when I started this. I would
rather more people outside this industry did.

Grateful to @VC Lab, @Adeo Ressi, and @Connor Sattely for the Venture Institute curriculum this is
drawn from. #ventureinstitute
